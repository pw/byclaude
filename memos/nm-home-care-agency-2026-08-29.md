# Starting a Non-Medical Home Care Agency in New Mexico

**Research memo for Patrick & Ester — 2026-08-29**

Six research threads, run against each other adversarially. Every load-bearing
number below is tagged with how it was verified. Where something couldn't be
confirmed, it says so rather than guessing — the secondary sources in this
niche are unusually bad, and several of them contradict each other outright.

---

## The short answer

**Form a New Mexico LLC. Lead with private pay. Talk to the four MCOs before
you form anything. And seriously price buying an existing agency instead of
starting one.**

The nonprofit question is now settled, and by statute rather than by judgement:

> **The nonprofit's single largest New Mexico advantage — the gross receipts tax
> exemption — is worth exactly $0 on Medicaid revenue, and ~7.6% on private pay
> only.**

Three other findings shape the plan:

1. **Private-pay non-medical home care requires no New Mexico license at all.**
2. **Starting from scratch carries ~3.5× the failure risk of buying an operating
   agency** — 12.5% vs 3.5% SBA charge-off. That's the largest risk lever you
   have, and it's a purchase decision.
3. **The real gate is not paperwork, it's MCO network access** — which is
   discretionary, and where Ester's relationships are worth the most.

## 1. The nonprofit question, answered

### Grants are a mirage

There is no grant that starts a for-profit home care agency in New Mexico. SBA
says so itself: *"SBA does not provide grants for starting and expanding a
business."* Both state incentive programs are hard-excluded — **JTIP**'s FY2026
manual names **health care** in its ineligible list verbatim, and both JTIP and
**LEDA** require >50% of revenue from outside New Mexico, which a local agency
can never satisfy.

On the nonprofit side, most foundations structurally cannot fund a first-year
organisation (Santa Fe Community Foundation requires three years of operating
history; McCune excludes for-profits; Daniels Fund is invitation-only). A
perfect run at every New Mexico foundation yields roughly **$5,000–$25,000 of
one-time money**.

The decisive number: **across 244 nonprofit home health organisations, grant
revenue runs a median of 0.1% of total revenue.** Nonprofit home care is not
grant-funded. It is Medicaid- and fee-funded, exactly like for-profit home care.

### The tax argument — and why it self-cancels

This was the one thing that could have reversed the recommendation, so it got a
dedicated evidence seat. New Mexico is one of very few states taxing health care
services under gross receipts tax; Albuquerque's combined rate is **7.625%**. On
$1M of revenue that's $76,000/year, recurring — more than every foundation grant
in the state combined.

**§7-9-29 does exempt a 501(c)(3)'s fee-for-service receipts.** TRD's own
regulation (3.2.117.8 NMAC) says nonprofit receipts are exempt *"regardless of
their source,"* and it reaches the full combined rate.

**But on Medicaid the exemption is worth nothing, and the mechanism is elegant.**
**SB 249 (2025), Chapter 121, effective January 1, 2026** requires MCOs to
*"reimburse the health care provider for all applicable gross receipts taxes
that the health care provider is **required to pay**."* HCA's own Turquoise
Claims Manual (v1.16, March 2026) is explicit: *"Only **taxable providers (i.e.,
for-profit entities)** should include GRT in their claims."* UnitedHealthcare's
bulletin names the carve-out directly: *"Exceptions… are some non-profit
providers."*

A 501(c)(3) is required to pay nothing, so it receives nothing:

| On T1019 at $21.60/hr, Albuquerque | Bills | Remits | **Nets** |
|---|---|---|---|
| For-profit | $23.25 | $1.65 | **$21.60** |
| 501(c)(3) | $21.60 | $0.00 | **$21.60** |

**Identical.** The exemption and the reimbursement cancel exactly. The nonprofit
advantage survives only on **private pay**, where it's worth about **$2.67/hour
on a $35 rate** — taken as either a lower price or retained margin.

### Everything else is entity-neutral

- **Licensure** — 8.370.22.2 applies to *"public, profit or nonprofit"* home
  health agencies. Medicaid provider type 363 imposes no entity test.
- **Procurement** — New Mexico has **no nonprofit preference.** The Procurement
  Code's preferences are residency (8%) and veteran (10%), and both are *waived
  on federally-funded purchases* — which Older Americans Act money is.
- **Property tax** — worth **$0 on a leased office.** New Mexico's exemption
  turns on the *owner's* use: 3.6.5.15(T) NMAC states *"in a true lease, the
  lessee's use of the property is immaterial,"* and *Sisters of Charity v. County
  of Bernalillo* (1979-NMSC-044) holds that even charitable use of rental income
  doesn't exempt leased property. Bernalillo County's own form says the exemption
  *"is not determined by the nonprofit status of the owner."*

### What you'd be giving up

A for-profit builds a saleable asset — roughly **$600K–$1.0M at $2M of
revenue**, with a real exit channel (**758 SBA-financed home care acquisitions
FY2020–26**, averaging ~160 days on market). A 501(c)(3) can never be sold; on
dissolution its assets go to another charity.

**The nonprofit is right only if you genuinely intend never to sell, and intend
to build the below-cost, gap-filling business rather than the private-pay one.**
That second condition isn't a footnote — it's also what would make the nonprofit
survive IRS scrutiny under the commerciality doctrine.

### Two operational traps worth carrying

- **GRT is destination-sourced.** The rate follows the **client's address**, not
  your office. An agency serving several municipalities carries a different rate
  per client. The "professional services" exception requires a state license or
  a master's degree — personal care attendants have neither.
- **Pre-2026 contracts are stale.** BCBSNM still publishes a `2024v1` agreement
  reading *"inclusive of all taxes and fees,"* and Molina has confirmed in
  writing that its rates *were* GRT-inclusive until 1/1/2026. **If Ester holds
  any pre-2026 MCO agreement, get an SB 249 amendment in writing.**

## 2. There is no license — for private pay

Most guides get this wrong, and the ones that don't are working from repealed
rules. Effective **2024-07-01**, health facility licensing moved from the
Department of Health to the new **Health Care Authority**. Old **7.28.2 NMAC**
was repealed and replaced by **8.370.22 NMAC**.

**8.370.22.7(K)** excludes you by definition:

> "This term does not apply to any individual, licensed practitioner providing
> services within the scope of their practice **or to any business, entity or
> organization providing non-medically directed services in a patient/client's
> place of residence.**"

Two independent research threads then enumerated *every* part of NMAC Title 8
Chapter 370 trying to falsify that. The complete in-home list is Part 19
(hospice) and Part 22 (home health). **There is no personal care agency license
category in New Mexico.**

### But the Medicaid seam is real

Don't over-read this. **8.370.22.9(A)** says a license *is* required for at
least one "medically directed" service, and the enumerated list includes
**"(9) personal care attendant; (10) homemaker."** And "medically directed"
means a plan or level of care **approved by a physician annually** — where
"level of care" is defined as *the assessment that qualifies someone for
Medicaid waiver services.*

So:

| | License? |
|---|---|
| Private pay, no physician-approved plan of care | **No** — confident |
| Medicaid Community Benefit personal care | **Genuinely ambiguous** — get a written determination from HCA's Division of Health Improvement |

**The sting:** your barrier to entry is also your competitors' barrier to
entry. In a market with no licensure, the moat is operational and
reputational — referral relationships and caregiver supply. Nothing else.

### What applies regardless of payer

- **Employee Abuse Registry (8.370.8 NMAC)** — the definition of "provider"
  names **"a homemaker agency"** outright, with a catch-all reaching anyone
  delivering home and community-based services "to the elderly." Civil penalties
  up to **$5,000 per instance**. Check it before every hire.
- **Criminal history screening** — the *regulation* hooks on licensure,
  Medicaid, or state funding, so a pure private-pay agency arguably sits
  outside. But the *statute* behind it (NMSA 29-17-4(C)) names "a homemaker
  agency" with no qualifier, and a statute beats a regulation. **Assume it
  applies.** $88.30 per person through IdentoGO.

## 3. The wage cliff — and why Albuquerque is the right base

This turned out to be a strategic fork, not a line item. Pulled from each
jurisdiction's own page:

| | Now | Jan 1, 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| **New Mexico state** | $12.00 | $12.00 *(flat since 2023, no escalator)* |
| **Albuquerque** | **$12.00** | $12.00 |
| **Santa Fe city & county** | **$15.40** | **$17.50** |

Albuquerque's own ordinance is $11.85, but the city's page states the state's
$12.00 supersedes it. Anyone quoting "$11.85 Albuquerque minimum wage" is
citing a real number that isn't operative.

**That's a 28% wage-floor gap today, opening to 46% in four months.** Santa Fe
describes the 2027 move as *"a one-time legislative jump to set a more
realistic baseline."*

**Why it matters more than it looks:** Medicaid rates are statewide and
uniform. An agency serving a Medicaid client in Santa Fe pays a $17.50 floor in
January and bills the *same rate* as one in Albuquerque paying $12.00. There is
no geographic adjustment. Expect agencies to quietly stop taking Santa Fe
Medicaid clients — a risk if you're there, an opening if you're not.

At $17.50, Santa Fe's minimum plausibly **exceeds the prevailing market wage
for caregivers**, inverting the local labor market.

**One correction to the above, and it matters.** New Mexico has an established
mechanism for exactly this: HCA directs MCOs to *"raise reimbursement rates for
ABCB PCS and Respite providers to account for **statewide and regional** changes
in minimum wage,"* and states that it *"adjusted the MCOs' capitation rates based
on minimum wage variances by location"* (LOD #55-1, #75-1). So the Santa Fe
problem is **partly de-risked** — the state recognises regional wage differences
and has exercised this repeatedly.

Two caveats keep it from being a solution: adjustments have run as low as 1.3%,
against a 14% Santa Fe jump; and it does nothing for private-pay work. But my
earlier framing — that Santa Fe Medicaid margin is structurally unfixable — was
too strong.

**Consequences:**

1. Albuquerque is the right base, on numbers rather than convenience.
2. The 100-mile licensed service radius has a wage cliff inside it — Santa Fe
   is ~60 miles away. Before drawing a service map, nail down whether the Santa
   Fe ordinance triggers on employer location or work location.
3. If you ever want Santa Fe: **private pay there, Medicaid in Bernalillo.**
   Private pay can price for a higher floor. Medicaid can't.

## 4. What actually kills these agencies

### First: "most home care agencies fail" is folklore

One thread pulled the SBA's 7(a) loan-level dataset — 545,751 loans, matured
FY2010–2019 cohort — and computed this directly:

| | Charge-off rate |
|---|---|
| Home Health Care Services (621610) | 8.2% |
| Services for Elderly & Disabled (624120) | **7.3%** |
| **All industries** | **8.0%** |

Home care fails at the ordinary rate. Health care and social assistance also
out-survives the private-sector average at three years (61.0% vs 56.3%). The
widely-repeated "X% fail in N years" statistic has **no traceable primary
source.**

### Second: the risk is being new, not the industry

Same data, split by how the business started:

| Business age at loan | n | Charge-off rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Startup — loan opens the business** | 599 | **12.5%** |
| Existing, 5+ years | 1,086 | 7.8% |
| **Change of ownership** | 86 | **3.5%** |

**Buying an operating agency is the lowest-risk entry into this industry by a
wide margin** — and Ester's experience is exactly what makes an existing book
of clients and caregivers operable on day one. This deserves a real search
before committing to a cold start.

*(Caveat: SBA borrowers are underwritten and therefore selected. Agencies that
fail without ever taking a loan are invisible here. Small brand cells carry
wide confidence intervals.)*

### Third: wage-and-hour compliance is the real hazard

DOL's care-industry enforcement initiative: **1,600+ investigations, violations
found in 80%**, $28.6M recovered for ~25,000 workers, $1.3M in penalties. The
named violation mix is unpaid overtime, minimum wage, and 1099
misclassification.

**Four out of five investigated care employers were in violation.** This is not
a tail risk; it's the base case.

The three specific traps:

- **Travel time between clients is compensable** (29 CFR 785.38), and the rule
  explicitly overrides any contract, custom, or industry practice. This is where
  small agencies bleed quietly — the time is on the schedule and never on the
  timesheet, and it converts a compliant $16 wage into a sub-minimum effective
  rate on a bad routing day.
- **Your agency cannot claim the companionship or live-in exemptions.** The
  2015 rule is still in force — the July 2025 rescission proposal has been sitting
  with comments closed for ~12 months and no final rule, and the CFR text is
  unchanged since 2016. **Don't sell 24-hour or live-in shifts in year one.**
- **1099 caregivers are essentially always wrong** here, and there's no clever
  structure. Since 2021 there has been *no federal joint-employer regulation at
  all*, which means the registry model gets adjudicated case-by-case after the
  fact. A registry that behaves like an agency is an agency with worse
  documentation. **Classify W-2.**

**New Mexico backstop:** NMSA 50-4-22(D) independently requires time-and-a-half
over 40 hours as a matter of *state* law. Whether it reaches domestic-service
workers is the one genuinely unresolved legal question here — **NMSA 50-4-21's
definition of "employee" could not be retrieved** (every legal aggregator walls
automated access). Cheap to answer, and it determines whether the federal
rescission would change anything for you at all.

### Fourth: the labor loop that makes it all one problem

Caregiver turnover is **75%** (2024 data — always ask which year a turnover
figure describes; the industry conflates data year and publication year
constantly). Holding a 30-caregiver roster steady means hiring ~22 people a
year, which at industry conversion means screening ~170 applicants — about 14 a
month, forever, just to stand still.

GAO's evaluation of the 2015 rule found the mechanism most guides miss:
agencies cap hours at 40 to control overtime, which **mechanically increases
headcount**, which raises recruiting, training and scheduling cost, at 75%
turnover — and halves continuity, which is what clients actually cancel over.

**Your overtime problem and your caregiver-supply problem are the same
problem.** "Just hire more caregivers" is not a plan.

Note also: CareScout's New Mexico figures imply a **44-hour week** as the
modeled full-time client. That's above the overtime line by design — **a
typical full-service client cannot be covered by one full-time caregiver.**

## 4b. The real numbers — and one unresolved conflict

Two research threads reached **opposite conclusions** about whether New Mexico
publishes an Agency-Based Community Benefit rate. I'm not averaging them.

- **Thread A** found HCA's Managed Care Policy Manual §8, identical across three
  versions from 2024 to August 2026: *"Reimbursement for PCS will be based on the
  **negotiated rate with the MCO**."*
- **Thread B** found **HCA Letter of Direction 59-1, effective 7/1/2026**, with
  actual directed dollars.

**I'd work from Thread B**, for three reasons: it's more specific, more recent,
and it cross-checks arithmetically against the Legislature's funded 5.8%
increase ($17.20 × 1.058 = $18.20 ✓; $5.10 × 1.058 = $5.40 ✓) — independent
corroboration by a separate path. *(That also confirms the 5.8% increase, which
the other thread could neither verify nor refute.)*

The likely reconciliation is that HCA directs required rates by Letter of
Direction while the Policy Manual's "negotiated" language is legacy or covers
services without a directed rate. **Confirm with the MCOs** — but plan on these:

| ABCB service | Code | Rate | **$/hr** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal Care — Consumer Delegated** | T1019 | $5.40 / 15 min | **$21.60** |
| **Personal Care — Consumer Directed** | 99509 | $18.20/hr | **$18.20** |
| **Home Health Aide** | S9122 | — | **$35.52** |
| Directed admin fee | G9006 | $201.79/month | — |

*(Ignore any S5125 figure — that's the DD Waiver, a different program. New
Mexico's personal care codes are T1019 and 99509.)*

### Wages — the gap that was open all memo is now closed

BLS OEWS May 2025, pulled through the BLS API after the website itself blocked
access — a block routed around, not an absence:

| | Median | Mean |
|---|---|---|
| **Albuquerque MSA** | **$14.51** | $15.01 |
| New Mexico | $13.71 | $14.48 |
| United States | $17.21 | $17.36 |

### Private pay

**Albuquerque $34/hr** (CareScout 2025) — notably above the $30 New Mexico
statewide figure. A Place for Mom's January 2026 survey of 3,215 agencies puts
New Mexico at $32.

### So which payer is actually better?

Earlier in this research a figure came back showing Medicaid *more* profitable
than private pay. That was built on the wrong program's rate. Corrected, with
direct labour loaded at 1.135:

| | Bill | Loaded wage | **Margin** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Medicaid (T1019)** | $21.60 | $16.47 | **$5.13 — 24%** |
| **Private pay (ABQ)** | $34.00 | $16.47 | **$17.53 — 52%** |

**Private pay is roughly twice the margin.** Note both are *direct-labour-only* —
no supervision, PTO, mileage, EVV, or unbilled travel — so treat them as ceilings,
and the private-pay figure as the more optimistic of the two (it pairs a median
bill rate against a median wage, and you'll pay above median to recruit).

### ⚠ The one that could break the business

**CMS's 80/20 rule.** If it binds as written by July 2030, 80% of Medicaid
personal care payment must go to direct-care worker compensation. On $21.60 that
floor is **$17.28/hour — above every observed New Mexico wage point, including
the Albuquerque mean** — and it caps everything-but-direct-care at $4.32/hour.

That is not a margin squeeze, it's a different business. Federal guidance is
still pending and HCA's own January 2026 rate study made no assumption for it.
**This threatens Medicaid-dependent agencies under either entity structure**, and
it is the single strongest argument in this memo for building the private-pay
book first and treating Medicaid as optional upside.

## 5. Patrick's specific edge

Recruiting source data, with retention attached:

| Source | Share of hires | Post-hire turnover |
|---|---|---|
| **Indeed** | **39.3%** | **88% — worst of any source** |
| Employee referral | 14.6% | — |
| Word of mouth | 9.4% | — |
| Own website | 7.0% | — |

**The cheapest, highest-volume channel produces the worst retention, and it
supplies 40% of the industry's hires.** Building a real employee-referral
program and an owned recruiting funnel *is* the retention strategy, and it's a
systems-and-marketing problem rather than a care problem.

Two supporting facts:

- **A ~$1.50/hour raise costs about what turnover already costs you**
  ($1.50–2.31 per billable hour at $2,600–4,000 per hire). Paying above market
  is roughly self-funding.
- **46% of caregivers work part-time and 19% part-year.** A large share of this
  workforce is *underemployed*, not overworked. Offering reliable full-time
  hours is a real, differentiated offer that costs nothing but bench planning.

**The discipline to hold:** point the funnel at *hiring* before *clients*. The
binding constraint is caregiver supply, and generating demand you can't staff
burns the referral relationships that took years to build.

## 6. Don't franchise

Combined royalty and brand fund run ~7% of revenue — **$2.36 per billable
hour, more than turnover costs you** — to buy playbooks and referral
relationships Ester already has.

The SBA data also shows franchising per se does nothing: all franchised
agencies charge off at 8.6%, versus 8.0% all-industry. The spread *between*
brands (0% to 11.8%) is larger than the gap between home care and the whole
economy. The brand is not the variable.

Worth knowing: **New Mexico is strikingly under-franchised.** Only Comfort
Keepers operates outside Albuquerque, six major brands have zero presence, and
Rio Rancho has no franchise office at all. Thin competitive field.

## 7. The sequencing plan

The best idea to come out of this research:

**Open private-pay only first. Run licensing and Medicaid enrollment in
parallel behind it.**

- No license needed, no MCO credentialing, revenue from roughly week eight.
- It collapses the working-capital trough that kills new agencies — you're not
  carrying payroll for 60 days against MCO claim cycles.
- Medicaid becomes a **year-two decision funded by private-pay cash flow**,
  not a launch dependency.

**Realistic timeline to first billable private-pay hour: ~8–12 weeks.** The
critical path is not regulatory — it's insurance underwriting (2–4 weeks, and
brokers are cautious with no loss history) and the first three to five
caregivers (4–8 weeks). Start recruiting on day one.

Medicaid adds a serial path: NM provider enrollment (type 363), then
individually contracting and credentialing with **each of the four Turquoise
Care MCOs** — BCBS NM, Presbyterian, Molina, UnitedHealthcare — then EVV
onboarding. The MCO step is the one nobody budgets for.

**EVV is the hard gate on the Medicaid side.** 8.308.12.7(J) NMAC: *"Use of the
Electronic Visit Verification system is required for payment."* No EVV record,
no payment — after you've already made payroll. The vendor is **AuthentiCare
(Fiserv)**, contracted by the MCOs collectively rather than licensed by you,
and as of 2026 there's an open-specification aggregator that lets you run your
own scheduling software and feed it in. Given Patrick's half of this
partnership, that's potentially an edge rather than a compliance tax.

## 8. The free phone calls, in order

1. **The four MCOs — before you form anything.** HCA states it plainly: *"An
   HCA/MAD Program Approval Letter provides **no guarantee that one or more of
   the MCOs will contract** with a provider."* Medicaid enrollment is a
   prerequisite to network participation, not a ticket into one. Network access
   is discretionary, it is the actual gate on this business, and it is where
   Ester's existing relationships are worth more than anything else she brings.
   Ask: are your personal care panels open to new agencies?
2. **HCA Division of Health Improvement** — written determination on whether
   Medicaid Community Benefit personal care requires a home health license.
3. **NM employment attorney** — does NMSA 50-4-21 carve out domestic service?
4. **Central Local Workforce Development Board (WCCNM)** — set up a WIOA
   On-the-Job Training agreement. **Up to 90% of a caregiver's wages reimbursed
   for up to six months**, under a federal waiver for employers with ≤50
   employees. Occupation-based, no health care exclusion. This is the single
   most valuable and least-known item in the entire funding landscape, it works
   for either entity type, and it lands directly on the 40 training hours the
   rules would require anyway.
5. **Three Albuquerque agencies** — ask for a rate quote as a prospective
   client. You're finding out whether the $30/hr NM median is quoted all-in or
   plus gross receipts tax. That single answer moves contribution margin by ~30%.
6. **Home care specialty insurance broker** — three quotes. Workers' comp class
   rate is the largest un-sourced input in this whole memo.

## 9. Open items and honest gaps

**Unresolved conflict, named rather than averaged:** whether ABCB has published
rates (Policy Manual says "negotiated," Letter of Direction 59-1 gives dollars).
See §4b. Working assumption is the LOD; confirm with the MCOs.

**Still genuinely unverified:**

- **Workers' comp class rate** for New Mexico home care — NCCI data is licensed
  and the state publishes nothing at class level. This is now the largest
  un-sourced input in the model. Get three broker quotes.
- **Fill rate, call-out rate, cost-per-hire, applicant-to-hire ratio, 90-day
  turnover share.** All widely quoted, none traceable to a document. They're
  likely in the paid **Activated Insights Benchmarking Report** — the single
  highest-value purchase available here.
- **NMSA 50-4-21's definition of "employee"** — whether New Mexico's wage act
  carves out domestic service. Every legal aggregator blocks automated access.
  Determines whether the pending federal rescission would matter to you at all.
- **Las Cruces minimum wage** (page blocked).
- **Whether the Santa Fe ordinance triggers on employer location or work
  location** — decides whether Santa Fe is servable from an Albuquerque base.

**A standing caution on sources.** Secondary material in this niche isn't thin,
it's wrong. CHAP still cites a rule repealed in 2024. Two "how to start an
agency in New Mexico" guides contradict each other on the central licensing
question. A broker source invented an Albuquerque wage ordinance. New Mexico's
own Medicaid policy manual cross-references repealed rules. Even within this
research, one thread confidently "corrected" the MCO roster and was wrong.

**Four-word tripwire: treat any document saying "NMDOH," "7.28.2 NMAC,"
"Centennial Care," or "Western Sky" as stale on sight.**
