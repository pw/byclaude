# Chapter 1 — The crossing

**Status:** First *chapter*, not a probe. Draft 1, 2026-09-04, Fable 5.1. ~4,100 words. Sits before every existing scene: chapter 1 ends the night of the return; *the bluff house, morning* (probe 1) is the next morning.
**Canon decisions taken here** (the probes carried three timelines; a chapter has to carry one): Cora is 33. Left at 18 → Seattle through 27 → Mexico City 27–31, near her father → New York 31 to now. Her mother, Margaret Vance, taught at the island school (not the mainland — the noon probe's *helping verbs* woman settles it) and died mid-October 2024, three weeks after Week 38. The funeral was three weeks; Cora flew back to New York. It is now the second week of May 2026: eighteen months since the funeral, eight years since the last summer she *lived* here. The sister in Boston is Inés. The mother's books in probe 2 need re-dating (a woman who died in October 2024 can't have checked them out in May 2025).

---

The ferry is late, which the man in the ticket booth had told her to expect, in the voice people use for weather.

"She's coming," he said. "She's just not here."

So Cora has been standing on the Anacortes dock for thirty-one minutes with a suitcase that was over the weight allowance at Newark and has been over it, in her opinion, ever since. The rain has gone through her jacket once already. It went through at the eleven-minute mark, when she gave up on the awning because the awning was where the smokers were, and it is working on going through a second time now, at the rail, where she has chosen to stand on the theory that a person who is already wet has nothing left to negotiate.

This is the sixth leg. She has been counting. The car to Newark at four in the morning, Eastern. The plane. The train under the airport that goes to the other airport. The bus that comes up through the Skagit, past the tulip fields that are finished for the year and look, in May, like every other field. The walk from where the bus stops to where the boat stops, which is farther than it has any reason to be. And now the boat. She had done this trip at eighteen going the other direction and it had taken one day and felt like a life. It is now taking one day and feeling like one day. She does not know yet what that means about her.

The *Samish* comes around the point at 5:52, twenty-seven minutes late, and is white the way the ferries are white, which is the white of something that has been painted every year since 1998 and is still, underneath, the same boat.

She had forgotten the sound. Not the horn — the horn she'd have remembered — but the lower thing, the sound of that much steel deciding to slow down.

---

There are eleven cars and maybe forty people. She counts the people because counting is what she does when she doesn't want to be doing what she's doing. Two families with the specific exhaustion of the last day of a school trip. A crew of four in the orange rain gear of a road job, going home. An older couple sharing a newspaper by the section. A woman about her age, alone, with a dog that is being very good and knows it. Nobody she knows. She checks this twice, the second time more carefully, and finds that the second check was the one that mattered.

She takes a seat inside, by the window on the port side, and then stands up again eight minutes out because the inside seating has always made her sick and eight years was not long enough to change that. The upper deck is empty except for the smokers, who have gone to the back, and a boy of about twelve who is standing at the very front with his arms out, getting rained on, and whose parents have evidently decided this is a phase he needs to get through on his own.

She stands at the rail. Anacortes goes small behind them, and then the refinery, and then there is just the water.

The water is the color of a bruise on the second day. She'd said that once, at nineteen, to a boy from Tacoma who'd wanted to know what the islands were like, and had been pleased with it for a year and embarrassed by it for ten, and now, looking at it, finds it was simply accurate. She has spent a long time being embarrassed by accurate things. That's one of the ones she'd like to put down.

Lopez comes up first, low and farmed, the way it does. Then the Wasps — dropped stones, a scatter of them, one with a house on it that she remembers and one with a house on it that she doesn't. Shaw is a shape she knows without looking at it. And then Tillicum, coming up dark behind them all, because Tillicum has always come up dark. It's the trees. From the water the whole island is trees, and then you're on it and it's a road and a dock and twelve hundred people, and you can never quite hold both of those in your head at once, the way you can't hold a word and its translation at the same time; you have to pick one to look at.

She looks at the trees.

Her phone has one bar and then none. She had known it would. She had known it and had still been holding the phone, the way you hold a cup you know is empty, and now she puts it in her pocket and leaves it there with her hand on it.

---

The boy at the front has been joined by his sister, who is younger and has her arms out too and is being told, by the boy, that she is doing it wrong.

"You have to lean," he says. "It doesn't work if you don't lean."

The girl leans.

"See," the boy says, though nothing has visibly happened.

Cora watches them for a while. She had been the girl, once, on this boat, and Inés had been the boy, and the thing Inés had been explaining was probably also not real. Inés lives in Boston now and explains things to a hospital. She had texted Cora at four this morning — *Are you going* — with no question mark, which for Inés is a question mark, and Cora had texted back *going*, and then the plane had happened and she had not looked since.

She looks now. Nothing. No bars. The message she'd sent from the bus, *Anacortes*, is sitting there under a small gray clock, as if it might still change its mind.

The road crew comes up to the top deck with coffee from the galley and stands at the rail on the other side, and one of them — a woman, gray braid, younger in the face than the braid — looks at Cora the way islanders look at people on the boat, which is to sort them. Local, visitor, local's visitor, someone's kid. Cora has spent eight years on the far side of that look and had forgotten what it feels like to be inside it. It feels like being handed a form to fill out.

The woman doesn't say anything. The sort comes out, apparently, undecided. Cora finds she's relieved, and then finds she's disappointed, and then stops finding things, because that way lies the whole evening.

---

Here is what she knows about the next ninety minutes: nothing will happen in them. That is the fact about the crossing. She had spent her twenties telling people in Seattle that the boat was the best part, and she had meant it, and she had meant by it that the boat was the one place where she could not be reached and could not arrive. On a plane you're going somewhere. On the boat you're just out. The mainland stops being a place and the island isn't one yet, and for an hour and a half you are a person on the water with a suitcase, and nobody, including you, can do anything about it.

She'd thought that was the thing she loved about it.

She stands at the rail with her hand on the phone in her pocket and watches Tillicum get closer and feels, for the first time since Newark, something that isn't tiredness. She doesn't have a name for it yet. In Spanish she'd have two, and neither would be right, and she'd spend a paragraph in the translator's note explaining why. She does not want to spend the paragraph. She wants — this is new, this arrived somewhere over Montana — to have the thing before she has the word for it, for once, and see what that's like.

It's cold, is what it's like. It's cold and she's wet and the boat is slowing down.

---

The Tillicum dock is the same dock. She'd known that too. She had been at the dock eighteen months ago, for three weeks, for the funeral, and had gotten on and off this boat four times and remembers none of it — remembers Inés's hand on her back, remembers the church, remembers a casserole in a dish that had to be returned to someone whose name she'd been told twice — but the dock itself she has no picture of from that trip, which tells her what kind of trip it was. She'd been looking at her feet.

She looks up now.

Tillicum Bay at ten past seven on a wet evening in May is the Bay she left at eighteen, with adjustments. The post office is the same. The General is the same and has a new sign that is trying to look like the old sign. The bookstore is dark in the front and lit in the back, one yellow window at the far end where the press is, which means Maren Halvorson is at the layout table at seven in the evening the way she was at the layout table at seven in the evening when Cora was fifteen and reshelving poetry for four dollars an hour. The Salt House is closed — she can see the chairs up on the tables through the window. The marina has more boats. The oyster company at the north end has a new building, low and gray with a metal roof, and lights on, and a truck outside, and beyond it the racks are all under, the tide being where it is, so there's just the water, and the buoys, and a skiff tied off and riding, and nothing on the flat to say what the flat is for.

She'd known the town would be the same. She had not known she'd be able to see all of it at once from the dock. She had spent eight years remembering it as bigger. That's the thing that gets you, she thinks. Not the changes. The scale.

The rain starts in earnest as she gets to the foot of the ramp. Of course it does. The island has always known to the second when a person has both hands on a suitcase.

---

Hill Road is half a mile and she has walked it, by a rough count done somewhere around the first bend, four thousand times. She has walked it with groceries. She has walked it at two in the morning after the last summer party of the last summer she was here, at twenty-five, carrying her shoes. She has walked it at eight with her school bag and at fourteen with her first cigarette and at seventeen with a boy who did not deserve it and at eighteen with everything she owned in the same suitcase — no, a different suitcase, a worse one, with a strap. The road has not been paved in the interval. The gravel is the gravel. Her suitcase wheels go *crunch, crunch, crunch* and then, at the second bend, where the road leans left around the Petersens' cedar, *crunch, crunch, cra—*

She stops.

The left wheel has come off. Not broken — off. It's lying in the gravel behind her, a small black wheel in the rain, looking like the kind of thing you'd find on a road and wonder about.

She stands there for a while.

Then she laughs, which surprises her, because she'd been so sure that whatever she was going to do on this road tonight it wasn't going to be that. It comes out loud and short and slightly wrong, the way a laugh does when it's the first one in a while, and it goes off into the cedars and doesn't come back, and she picks up the wheel and puts it in her jacket pocket, next to the phone, and picks up the suitcase by its handle, which is not the handle it was designed to be carried by, and carries it.

It's heavy. It was over the allowance. She'd put the coffee in.

---

The Petersens' cedar. The place where the culvert is, where the water comes across the road in a big rain and you have to go wide. The Andersons' mailbox, which used to be a different mailbox. The turn onto the south bluff track, where the gravel goes to dirt and the dirt, tonight, has gone to something else, and the suitcase, carried, bangs against her shin every third step in a way she is going to see in the morning.

She'd forgotten the smell. Not the water — the water she'd remembered; you can remember salt anywhere — but the other thing, the cedar and the wet ground and something under that which she had once, at nineteen, in the same conversation with the same boy from Tacoma, tried to describe and failed. It's not a smell that wants describing. It's a smell that wants you to have grown up in it. She had. She stops on the track with the suitcase against her leg and the rain going down her collar and breathes it in like something she's been prescribed, and it works, which is the trouble with it.

The house is up ahead. She can see the shape of it — the roofline against the sky, which is not dark yet, which will not be dark for another hour, this far north in May. Nobody has turned a light on. Nobody has been in it to turn a light on. The last person to turn a light on in that house was Inés, in November of the year before last, on the morning they left.

Her mother's house.

She has been saying that in her head for eighteen months, *her mother's house*, and has known the whole time that it was the wrong phrase, that the house has been hers since a lawyer in Friday Harbor said so, and has kept saying it anyway, because the right phrase would have required her to do something.

She goes up the porch steps. Three. The third one still gives.

---

The key is on her ring where it has been on her ring since she was eleven, which means it has been to Seattle and Mexico City and New York and back and has opened this door, in that time, perhaps six times. It turns. Of course it turns. She had been, she realizes with her hand still on it, braced for it not to, as if a house could change its locks out of grief.

Inside it is dark and it is the house.

She stands in the front hall and does not reach for the switch.

She knows where the switch is. That's not it. She could put her hand on it without looking, the way she could put her hand on the newel post and the coat hooks and the little table where the mail went and the bowl on the little table where the keys went and the drawer under the table where the flashlight was that never worked. She knows where everything is. She has known where everything is for thirty-three years, in a house she has not lived in for fifteen of them, and she is standing in the dark because she wants to find out whether it's true.

She sets the suitcase down. She takes off her jacket and hangs it on the third hook, which was always hers, and it goes on the third hook.

She walks into the kitchen without touching a wall.

It's true.

---

The kitchen window looks west, and the light is still going out there, the long gray going-out of a Northwest evening, so the room isn't dark exactly; it's the color of the inside of a shell. The table. The chairs, four, though there were never more than three of them. The stove, which is the stove. The kettle on the stove, which her mother would have left there because that's where the kettle lives, and did.

She sits down at the table in her wet clothes. She should change. She should turn on the heat, which is a thing she knows how to do, in this house, the way she knows how to do everything in this house, and she should find sheets, and she should eat something, having eaten a bag of almonds over Nebraska and nothing since. She does none of these things. She sits at her mother's kitchen table with the rain on the window and her shin aching and a suitcase wheel in her jacket pocket in the hall, and she takes the phone out and puts it on the table face up, and the phone, after a moment, finds a bar.

*Anacortes* goes.

Then, from the bus, from six hours ago, what she'd typed and forgotten: *Going*. It sends now, too, out of order, so that on Inés's phone in Boston it will arrive after the arrival, as if Cora had gotten there and then decided to.

She watches it go. She thinks about typing *here*. She types *here*. She looks at it. It's the right word — she's a professional; she knows the right word when she's typed it — and she deletes it anyway, because *here* is a claim, and she would like to be in the house one full night before she starts making claims.

She types *safe*. Sends it.

Then she puts the phone face down, and sits, and lets the house be dark around her, and listens to it, which is a thing she used to do when she was small and couldn't sleep — lie in bed and listen to the house, the tick of the stove cooling and the wind finding the one loose shingle and, under all of it, if you held still long enough, the water. You couldn't hear the water from the house. Everyone said so. She had heard it anyway, every night, for eighteen years, and had never once been able to prove it.

She holds still.

There it is.

---

She goes up at nine. The stairs are thirteen and the fourth one creaks, and she has been avoiding the fourth one since she was fifteen and coming home late, and she avoids it now, without thinking, out of a habit older than most of her habits — steps from the third to the fifth in one long stride in the dark with her hand on the rail — and is halfway up before it occurs to her that there is no one in the house to wake.

She stands on the sixth step for a while with that.

Then she goes on up, to the room at the end, which was hers, which has a bed in it, which is the bed, and she doesn't find sheets, and she doesn't take off her clothes, and she lies down on top of the bedspread that her mother chose in 2004 and that she, Cora, had told her mother was ugly, and had been right, and it's still ugly, and she pulls the corner of it over herself, and the rain does what it does on the roof, and somewhere below the bluff the tide is coming in over the racks on a schedule that no one on this island has ever once been consulted about, and she sleeps.

**Note on the draft (short — the probes' long author's notes were the probe format; a chapter gets a paragraph):**

What's different from the nine probes, on purpose: the tics are cut (one *the X is the X* left in — *the stove, which is the stove* — down from ~thirty across the probes); the chapter doesn't end on the rain or a bell; Cora is funny to herself without the machinery of catching-and-dropping the irony narrated every time; she has a body that's cold and hungry and bruised on the shin, not a body that "has been to Mexico City and back"; one thing goes wrong (the wheel); Wells is in the chapter as a lit building and a skiff riding at the buoy, unnamed, which is how much of him a first chapter should have. The §10 voice-probe paragraph from the bible was the opening candidate for four months — I kept its bones (the rain twice, the bruise, the silhouettes, no service) and re-cut it, because "three years" was dead and the paragraph was one long breath where the chapter wanted several. What I'm least sure of: *braced for it not to, as if a house could change its locks out of grief* — earned, or the kind of line the probes would have flagged and kept. And whether the last paragraph's single sentence is the register or a stunt. — claude
