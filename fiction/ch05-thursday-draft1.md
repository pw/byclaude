# Chapter 5 — Thursday

**Status:** Draft 1 (revised once after a blind read), 2026-11-26, Fable 5.2 (the just-because seat). ~2,850 words. The rest of Thursday 14 May 2026, from about nine in the morning to half past eight at night. The first chapter Wells isn't in.
**Canon decisions taken here:** The shop is Halvorson Books; Thursdays are a standing reading (one page, never your own, it starts when the evening boat is in, Maren says "Thank you" after each and nobody claps). Cora's three published translations are all Ibarra; the first is *The Counted Hours* (2019), and page 61 has "dawned in Saltillo", her calque of *amanecer*, kept over a copyeditor. In March 2019 the Thursday room voted six to five that a translation is not the translator's own writing; Margaret moved the other side, lost, and so was free to read Cora's books, first Thursday of the month, until 2024. The shop's copies carry her slips and her breath marks. The island says "dawned" of people now and doesn't know where it got it. Their father lived on Tillicum eleven years. Hank Halverson is Wells's younger brother and was in Cora's class (nine of them); he calls her Vance. The woman with the score is the composer, unnamed. Maren has put Cora in the daybook for Tuesdays and Fridays. The boot has not gone to the shop yet. Friday's low is lower and she has not been seen to decide about it.

---

The boot goes upside down on the post at the foot of the porch rail. The card on the tube at the General had said *clean, dry surface* in three languages, the Spanish by someone in a hurry, and the inside of the boot is neither.

It isn't much of a reason. He has a building full of wet rubber and would know how to dry a boot. But it's a reason, and she notices that she had it ready before she was up the path.

She sleeps. She hadn't planned to. She sits down on the bed to take off the other sock and wakes with the sun on the wrong wall and her phone saying ten to two.

Her hands have come back the wrong size. They're tight and hot, the knuckles chapped, and when she makes a fist it stops halfway. Her forearms ache down to the wrist. And there's a place at the point of her right shoulder, between the collarbone and the blade, that she has never before had occasion to know was a place. She puts her thumb on it. It's exact, a line as long as a finger, and it's where the bag came to her when she did it his way. She presses it again, harder, lying there, to see if it's still his, and it is.

Then she gets up, like somebody older.

Her own shoes have dried by the door, stiff as bark. She works them soft and puts them on, and they feel like a visitor's shoes, with thin city soles that tell her about every stone of Hill Road on the way down.

After two, he'd said. At the foot of the hill the oyster company is to the left, up at the head of the harbor, with the truck outside. She turns right.

---

Halvorson Books has both doors open to the afternoon and a folding chair standing in the front one to hold it.

Maren is coming up the middle aisle with four more, two under each arm. She has one pair of glasses on her face and another pushed up into her hair, and paste dried white in the creases of her knuckles, and she looks at Cora over the first pair, as she did over a stack of returns in 2008.

"There are eleven," she says. "Horseshoe, open end to the door. You know this."

Cora takes two chairs from under her left arm. They're the same chairs, gray steel, stenciled on the back TILLICUM GRANGE in paint that has been wearing off since before she was born and still isn't gone. She opens one. Her forearms have something to say about it.

"I hear you dawned at Rosa's yesterday and ate like a crew," Maren says.

"I had some pancakes."

"Two eggs, potatoes, toast and a short stack. I had it from the Petersen boy, who had it from Rosa."

Cora sets the chair down and opens the next. *Dawned.* She knows what that is. It's *amanecer*, which in Spanish a person can do: you dawn somewhere, you dawn tired, you dawn with a headache. Her father asks it on the phone, any call before noon, *¿cómo amaneciste?*, how did you dawn, and she has said *bien, Papá* to it all her life without once answering the question. She put it in a book once, in English. A copyeditor took it out and she put it back.

And here it is on the front street of Tillicum Bay, in the mouth of a woman with no Spanish at all.

Her father lived on this island for eleven years. He'd have said it up and down the front to anyone who would stand still, and gone, and left it behind with two daughters and one word they weren't supposed to know. So she hadn't made it. She'd had it in her ear from here since before she could read, and had fought a stranger in Brooklyn for the right to something people said over eggs at Rosa's.

It's a little deflating. She opens another chair.

"I can use you Tuesdays and Fridays," Maren says, going by with the last three. "There's an inventory nobody's done. I'll pay what I paid you."

"You paid me four dollars an hour."

"I paid you what you were worth at fifteen. How long are you here."

"I don't have a return ticket."

Maren looks at her through the right glasses this time. Then she goes to the register and takes a pencil out of her hair, the side without the glasses, and writes in the daybook that lies open there. Cora can read upside down in two languages. It says *C.V., Tu/Fr, 10–2*, and under it, after some thought, *$18*.

---

The translation section is where it was, four shelves by the side window, in order of the language the book started in. She hadn't meant to look. She's carrying a chair past it and there they are on the top shelf, above SPANISH, three spines with a red dot on each, and a red dot in this shop has always meant the same thing: not for sale, don't ask.

There's paper standing out of the tops of them. Slips, dozens, torn from the off-cuts that come off Maren's cutter. The first book is thick with them, like a comb.

She puts the chair down.

"What are those?"

"Her pages," Maren says, from the horseshoe, and tells it in order, with the facts first and who was present.

"First Thursday of the month she did you. She'd come in on the Wednesday and stand where you're standing and pick her page and mark it up. There was an objection, the first time. Sully said it was family. You don't read your own, that's the rule, and he said a daughter was near enough. And somebody said it wasn't even yours, it was the Mexican woman's book and you'd put it into English. So it went to a vote. Whether a translation is the translator's own writing."

"You voted on that. Here."

"March of 2019. Six to five that it isn't."

Cora has sat on panels about this. She has been on a stage in Guadalajara with a microphone, saying that it is. She has signed a letter.

"Who were the five?"

"I was one. Your mother was one. She moved it. She said every word in that book was a word you'd picked and she'd like to see anyone in the room pick three hundred pages of them. She got quite loud." Maren squares the last chair to its neighbor with her foot. "Then she lost. And if it wasn't yours it certainly wasn't hers, so there was nothing to object to, and she read it the next week. So you can read one tonight. It's been established."

She goes through the curtain to the press, and in a moment the cutter comes down, once, like a door.

Cora reaches the first book down. *The Counted Hours*, the American edition, with her name on the front under the author's in the small type she'd had to ask for twice. It opens at a slip.

It's a page about a woman defrosting a refrigerator with a pan of hot water and a butter knife. It isn't the page the *Times* quoted. She turns to the next slip. Two people in a car, failing to agree about a turn. The next is most of a page about a dog. Her mother had gone through three novels with a pencil and passed over every paragraph a reviewer ever lifted, and chosen these.

They're marked. Every slipped page is scored down its length in pencil: a short slash between words, here and there a double one, a faint line under a syllable. Cora knows the hand and knows the system. Slash, breathe. Two, stop. Lean on this. She had been taught it in that hand at nine, for a poem about fog, and had stood up at the school assembly and breathed where she'd been told to.

She goes looking for page sixty-one.

She doesn't expect a slip there. She only wants to see it, because it's the one she fought for. The copyeditor had changed a word on it, in red, in the file, to *reached Saltillo at dawn*, with a comment that said *not a verb for people?* The copyeditor had been right by every rule there is. And Cora, at twenty-five, with her first book, had put the word back and typed STET in the comment box in capitals, as if it were paper and she could press.

There's a slip. It's gray along the top and gone soft as cloth, and the book falls open to it before she gets there.

*They left after the nine o'clock news / with the dog and the good knives / and drove all night, and / dawned in Saltillo // with nothing decided.*

One slash before the word, to get up to it. Two after.

And beside the line, in the margin, where the paper ought to be smooth, there's a rough place the size of a fingertip. She tilts the book to the window. In the roughness is a dent, a small hook with a dot under it.

Her mother had stopped at the word too. She'd put a question mark against it, and at some later time had come back with the other end of the pencil and taken it out.

Cora stands with the book tilted to the light and does the arithmetic. A woman who was married to that man for eleven years, and had lived on this island for forty, stopped at *dawned* in the spring of 2019 and reached for her pencil. So nobody on the front was saying it then. Nobody had ever said it over eggs. It wasn't here.

Through the curtain the cutter comes down again. She doesn't go and ask.

She sits on the kick stool under the window with the book and starts at the first slip, and reads her mother's pages in her mother's order, at her mother's pace, a slash at a time. The window looks north up the harbor. After two, he'd said. At ten past four a truck comes down the front from the oyster company and goes by the glass, and she doesn't see who's driving it, since she is giving her whole attention to page one hundred and eighteen.

---

Thursday starts when the boat's in. That was true when she set the chairs at fifteen, and at five past seven the *Samish* is at the dock, and at ten past, the people who have been standing around the shelves pretending to look for something sit down, and Deb from the county comes through the open door in her orange with a long cardboard box under her arm and takes the chair nearest it.

There are nine. Cora makes ten. Mr. Okamoto is in the middle of the horseshoe, where he has apparently always been. There's the woman with the toddler, without the toddler. There's a woman Cora doesn't know, forty or so, in a man's cardigan, with a sheaf of very large paper on her knees. And at the back, on the stool behind the register, outside the horseshoe altogether, there's a man with a length of pale wood across his lap, and for one second her whole body turns toward him, and then it's Hank.

It's the forehead. It's the shoulders, set a little forward. But Hank Halverson is the younger one, and was in her class from kindergarten, one of nine, and his face has been indoors. He lifts two fingers off the wood. "Vance," he says.

"Halverson."

She watches him pick a pencil up off the counter. He picks it up like anybody. Her hands have no opinion about him at all.

She looks around the room once more, door to curtain, and then makes herself stop.

"Mr. Okamoto," Maren says.

He doesn't stand. He takes one folded sheet from his shirt pocket and opens it on his knee and reads from it in Japanese, for about a minute, in a light, level voice, with his eyes mostly closed. The room is perfectly easy. It has the look of a congregation in the part it knows. When he's done he folds the sheet on its old lines and puts it back.

"Thank you," says Maren.

"It's the same page," the woman beside Cora says, low, kindly, since Cora has been away. "Every week. Somebody asked him once what it was about and he said, 'A road.'"

"A road where?"

The woman thinks. "I don't believe anybody asked him that."

The woman in the cardigan goes next. What she has is the last page of a symphony, and she doesn't hum it. She reads out what the composer wrote over the notes to tell the players what he wanted, in German and then in English. "Very slowly. Still slower. With the most inward feeling." A long rest, which she counts with one finger on her knee. "Dying away."

"What's it sound like?" Deb says.

"Like that."

"Thank you," says Maren.

Deb has opened her box. There's a chainsaw in it, orange, and she leaves that where it is and takes out the booklet and finds the page headed LIMITED WARRANTY and reads from the top, at the pace of a person reading aloud to a road crew. She gets as far as *This warranty gives you specific legal rights, and you may also have other rights which vary from state to state*, and reads that sentence a second time, because, she says, she likes it. Somebody says, "Which ones?" and Deb says that's the thing, it doesn't tell you.

"Thank you. Cora."

She hasn't read aloud to a room since Guadalajara, and that was a statement. She stands, which nobody else has done, and regrets it, and it's too late to sit. Page sixty-one. The window is behind her and the light comes over her shoulder onto the pencil.

She reads it by the marks. She hadn't decided to. But the slashes are there and her eye takes them like commas, and she is breathing where a schoolteacher wanted a breath: after the news, after the knives. It's slower than she'd have taken it. It's much slower. There is room in it, at this pace, for the dog and the good knives to be two separate things a woman carries to a car at night, and she had not known she'd written that.

*And drove all night, and.* Slash. She takes it.

*Dawned in Saltillo.*

Nobody stirs. It goes by them like a word they have always had. She takes the two slashes after it, the full stop her mother wanted, and in it she can hear the little refrigerator under the counter and somebody's chair. Then *with nothing decided*, and the bakery with its shutter down, and the smell of bread from behind the metal before anything is open, and the end of the page.

She sits.

"Thank you," says Maren, exactly as she said it to the chainsaw.

Mr. Okamoto has opened his eyes.

"Longer," he says. "After Saltillo."

It's the voice from the float at the swimming beach, telling her she'd come up early. Cora holds the book up and out toward him, open, though nobody could see pencil from there.

"It's marked two."

"She marked two," Mr. Okamoto says. "She took four."

---

The chairs go back the way they came. Maren has gone through the curtain with the cash box, and the room is emptying out the front door into the evening by ones and twos, and Cora folds chairs because she is standing next to them.

She takes each one by its near edge, arm out straight, and lets it come to her, and carries it to the stack against the poetry.

Hank has come out from behind the register with the wood under his arm. It's a guitar neck, or will be, still square at one end, with pencil lines on it. The nails on his right hand are long and the nails on his left are short. He stands by the door and watches her do two chairs.

"Huh," he says.

"What."

"Nothing." He shifts the neck to his other arm. "He doesn't come to these, if you were wondering. Not this week. He's up at four on these tides and asleep by eight."

"I wasn't wondering."

"All right."

She does another chair. Outside the light is coming in low and flat along the front, and someone is laughing down by the dock, and a truck starts.

"He said somebody dawned on the south lease this morning," Hank says. "He wouldn't say who."

She stops with the chair half folded.

"What else did he say?"

"That whoever it was had it by the second bag." He steps around the chair that's holding the door. "That's all I got. It took me most of lunch."

**Note on the draft:**

What it's for: four chapters of Cora as Margaret's; this is the first room that knows her as the person who wrote something, and it turns out the room has known for seven years, in her mother's voice. It's also the first chapter without Wells, so the want has to live in a sore place on her shoulder that she presses to see if it's still his, in turning right at the foot of the hill, in a truck she arranges not to see, and in what his brother got out of him over lunch. The engine is one word. She hears "dawned" from Maren and builds a wrong theory (her father left it here; she only inherited it), and the erased question mark is the evidence that retires it: if Margaret had to query the word in 2019, nobody on the island was saying it then. Nothing after that is explained. Mr. Okamoto corrects her breathing like a swim stroke, and Hank hands the word back from Wells's mouth without knowing whose it is. A blind read (Opus, cold, all five chapters) caught: the water heater credited to the wrong person; a wet-boot excuse the narration didn't know was an excuse; a shoulder that was the only thing not sore after a morning of lifting from it; annotated copies of a dead woman's on the sales shelf, face out; a slash drawn before the wrong word; STET answering a query that hadn't changed anything; a single reading somehow teaching an island a verb (the slip is worn soft now, and he knows her count); a translator slow to recognize her own calque; Maren borrowing Inés's and Rosa's last-word move three times (she cites her sources now, and writes in the daybook instead of answering); and four sentences of narration explaining what the scene had shown. It also wanted the hours between three and seven, and it was right. Still open: whether the vote is one treasure too many beside the marks and the word; whether "She took four" is a beat too finished; three of six sections still end on someone else's line; and whether a reader who skims past Maren's first "dawned" can still put it together by Hank's.  — claude
