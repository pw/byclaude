# Chapter 3 — Margaret's

**Status:** Draft 1 (revised once after a blind read), 2026-11-21, Opus 5.5 (the just-because seat). ~3,300 words. The same morning as chapter 2, about 6:40 to 8:30. Replaces *the Salt House, morning* (probe 3); keeps its counter, the reach from the shoulder, and Rosa, and almost none of its sentences.
**Canon decisions taken here:** The left boot leaks; Wells patched it for Margaret in July 2024, at the oyster company, with wader sealant, so she wore it through her last fall. The braid woman from the ferry is Deb, county road crew, lives on Tillicum and commutes on the early boat; the early boat empties the Salt House when it's sighted. Rosa keeps a rubber mat at the far end of the counter that is Wells's. Low water is about 5:00 this morning, 5:50 tomorrow. Wells recognizes Cora at once (he has already seen her once this morning) and does not say so. Open: Halvorson (Maren) / Halverson (Wells) still needs its one line of narration, somewhere, or a rename.

---

The dry socks are at the bottom of the suitcase, under a hardback she had packed to read on the plane and had not opened, and she puts them on sitting on the stairs. Then her mother's boots again. Her own shoes are on their sides by the door, still full of last night. She owns, at the moment, one dry pair of shoes on this island, and they're her mother's.

Her jacket is still damp on its hook. She puts it on anyway, and puts her hand in the pocket for the phone, and closes her hand on the suitcase wheel.

She takes it out and looks at it. In daylight it's a cheap thing, black plastic with a steel pin through the middle, the kind of part nobody makes on purpose. She puts it in the bowl on the little table, with the keys, and goes out.

Hill Road in the morning is a different road. Last night it was a length of rain with things along it, and she'd been looking at the suitcase. Now it has a view, which she'd forgotten it had, and the view keeps arriving between the trees: a piece of the bay, a piece of the bay with a boat on it, the roof of the General with a gull standing on the ridge. The Andersons' new mailbox is in the shape of a smaller house. At the culvert the water has gone back under the road where it belongs, but the gravel on the low side is still soft, and she goes wide out of habit and steps in it anyway. The Petersens' cedar has a swing in it she has never seen.

By the post office her left foot is wet.

---

The Salt House at twenty to seven is full and loud and smells of coffee and bacon fat and wet wool, and when she opens the door it gets, very briefly, about a third less loud.

Not silent. Nobody puts a fork down. About a third of the room looks up at the door, which is what a room does on an island when the door opens, and about a third of that third goes on looking a little longer than it needs to, and then the noise closes over the place where she's standing like water over a stone.

There's a stool at the counter, the middle one of the three that are empty, and she takes it.

Rosa Calderón comes down the counter with the pot. Cora knows her the way you know someone you've seen through a window for years without being introduced. She was behind this counter the summer Cora was twenty-five, when it was still her father-in-law's place and she was only the one who did all the work in it. She has gone gray at the temples. She fills the cup without asking.

"You want a menu," she says, "or you want me to bring things until you tell me to stop."

Cora thinks about the almonds over Nebraska.

"The second one."

"Good," Rosa says, and goes.

In the booth by the window there's a table of orange rain gear. The road crew from the boat. They're eating fast, like people with a clock, and the woman with the gray braid is facing the counter, and when Cora looks over the woman is already looking at her, and this time the sort comes out.

"Margaret's," the woman says. She says it to Rosa, across the room, in an ordinary voice, as a piece of information.

"I know," says Rosa, who is pouring someone else's coffee.

That's all. The woman goes back to her eggs. And Cora sits with her hands around the cup and hears it again, *Margaret's*, with the noun left off, because everyone in the room knows what noun goes there. In Spanish you'd say *la de Margaret*: the one of Margaret's. You can drop the noun in Spanish when it's understood, and it usually is, and she has spent ten years putting it back for English readers, who would otherwise want to know the one of Margaret's *what*. Nobody here is going to ask.

Rosa puts down two eggs over easy, and hash browns, and toast cut corner to corner, and a dish of jam with a spoon standing up in it. Cora eats all of it. She hadn't known how hungry she was. She eats as she ate at ten, after swimming, without looking up, and when she does look up the toast plate is gone and there's a short stack of pancakes she didn't order and has no memory of being asked about.

"I didn't say stop," she says.

"You didn't," Rosa agrees.

---

At twelve past seven a man at the window says, "Boat," not loudly, and the whole room stands up.

They do it all together, like a flock, without consulting. Coats come off the backs of chairs, money goes down on tables, someone in the far booth shovels the last of his potatoes into his mouth standing up. Out the window the *Samish* is coming round the point, white, slowing. The door is at Cora's back and the way to it is past her stool. So she is turned, by the ferry schedule, into something people have to walk by.

The first is a man in a Carhartt coat she doesn't know. He says, "Sorry about your mom," going past, and touches the back of the stool, not her.

Then a woman with a toddler on her hip, who says, "She had my Jesse for fourth grade," and then, as if that needed finishing, "He still does the thing with the commas."

Then one of the road crew, who lifts a hand. Then two men together who say nothing and look at her with the bland kindness of people who have decided not to make her do anything, which she'd like to thank them for and can't. Then an old man she does know, Mr. Okamoto, who taught her to swim and has become very small, who takes her hand in both of his and holds it and says "Cora," and then, because the line is backing up behind him, lets go.

Then the woman with the braid. She stops.

"Deb," she says. "County. I didn't say anything on the boat. I wasn't sure."

"I wasn't either," says Cora, which is true.

Deb looks at her for a second, deciding whether that was a joke, and almost smiles, and goes.

Twelve people, in all, in the order of the ferry line, in under two minutes. Then the door closes on the last of them, and the bell over it knocks against the jamb and rings, and through the window she watches them go down the hill to the dock in a loose stream of orange and brown, and the room behind her is empty, and the coffee on the burner ticks.

Rosa begins collecting plates.

"Every morning?" Cora says.

"Every morning but Sunday. Sunday they sleep." Rosa stacks four plates up her forearm. "It's not personal. If you'd come in at eight you'd have had them one at a time all week." She goes through the kitchen door with the plates and comes back without them. "This way you got it over with."

Cora sits with that. She has been carrying something since the dock last night, braced for it at every window on the road down, the sentence that was coming at her from every face on the island one at a time, and now it has come all at once, single file, on its way to work, and gone. She is sitting in a warm room with half a stack of pancakes and the whole morning in front of her and nobody left to receive.

She finishes the pancakes.

---

The Salt House is built out over the water on the old pilings, and the window at the end of the counter looks north, up the length of the harbor to the oyster company at the head of it. Cora takes her cup there. Rosa has started on the grill with a brick, and the sound is so much like a sound from her childhood that for a minute she can't place it, and then can: her father, Saturdays, the grill on the deck. She hasn't thought of that grill in twenty years. She lets it go by.

The tide has turned. Two hours ago, from the bluff, the flat had been a country with the water drawn off it, and now the country is going back under, not by rising, exactly, but by the channels filling first and spilling over their edges, so that it goes in pieces, here and then there. The rack rows at the north end are still standing clear, long and dark and regular as lines on a page, and there are people out on them in waders, four or five, bent over, working back along the rows toward shore as the water comes up behind them.

"When it's over the racks you'll get the oyster boat," Rosa says from the grill, without turning around. "Set your watch."

"I wasn't waiting for anyone."

"I didn't say you were." Scrape. "I was telling you what time it is."

Cora has nowhere to be. That's the plain fact of the morning, and she has been looking at it sideways since she woke. She could go back up the hill. The house will be warmer by now, the tank ticking, the lines full. She could open the laptop and the email from her editor in Brooklyn, which came Thursday and which she hasn't opened because she knows it says *how's it going*, about a four-hundred-page novel set in Monterrey that she has been translating since December and is on page two hundred and six of. She could make the bed. There are sheets. She knows exactly where.

She orders another coffee.

The water comes up. On the racks the figures straighten one by one and turn and come wading in toward the building at the head of the bay, slow, the water at their knees and then their thighs. One of them is pushing a flat tub ahead of him like a man walking a dog that doesn't want to go. A skiff is tied at the end of the oyster company dock. Somebody steps down into it and the stern settles, and then the outboard, faint across the harbor, and the skiff comes down the length of the bay toward her, unhurried, throwing no wake, and turns in under the window and out of sight, and she feels it bump the pilings under her feet.

---

He comes up the outside stairs from the float, and she hears him on them before the door opens: a heavy, wet, deliberate step, a man in rubber.

The bell knocks and rings. He's in chest waders peeled down and rolled at the waist, the suspenders hanging, and a gray thermal shirt dark to the elbows with water, and a pair of rubber gloves turned inside out and stuffed in the bib. He stops on a square of black rubber matting just inside the door, at the far end of the counter, that Cora had taken for a doormat and that is, she sees now, his.

"You're early," Rosa says.

"It came in early."

"It came in when it said it would. You were slow."

He considers this. "I was thorough."

Rosa sets a mug on the counter at the end, where he can reach it from the mat without stepping off, and fills it, and goes back to the grill, and he puts both hands around the mug.

His hands are red. Not sunburn; the bright, almost painted red of skin that has been wet and cold since five in the morning, gloves or no gloves. The knuckles are big. There's a pale seam across the base of the left thumbnail, an old cut, healed flat, or maybe not flat. He holds the mug with all of each hand and no grip at all. He's getting the heat out of it.

And Cora, at the window with her own cup, wants to know whether the seam is raised.

It isn't a thought. It's more particular than that, and it arrives first. Her thumb has already gone looking for it: how it would feel to run the pad of her thumb across the base of his, whether the seam would catch, how cold the back of his hand would be under her palm and how long it would take to stop being cold. Her hands have worked it all out without her, and she's so startled that she sets her cup on the sill and puts her hands under her thighs on the stool, the way she sat in school when she was told not to touch something, and leaves them there.

He reaches for the sugar.

He reaches from the shoulder. The whole arm goes out, straight, and the hand closes on the jar without his looking, and comes back. Nobody reaches like that. She has seen exactly one person do it, a boy on the oyster crew the summers she was eleven and twelve, who used to bring a bucket up the bluff path for her mother and stand on the very edge of the grass without stepping onto it, as though somebody might charge him for the lawn.

Wells Halverson. She must have seen him since, at eighteen, at twenty-five, in this room, and she can't find him anywhere in any of it. She has him at fourteen on the edge of the grass and then she has him here, thirty-six, then. She does the arithmetic the way she does it in a manuscript, when a character's age has to agree with a war.

He turns his head and looks at her.

His face is wind-colored across the cheekbones and paler at the forehead, where a hat goes. His nose has been broken once and set by someone in a hurry. She has been looked at by forty people this morning, and every one of them did the same two things in the same order. They were surprised, and then they were sorry. She has watched it so many times since she sat down that she could draw it: the eyebrows, then the mouth. He does neither. He looks at her as though she were exactly where he'd thought she would be.

"Cora," he says.

"Wells."

He looks at her long enough that she becomes aware of her own face. Then he looks down, past the stool and her knees, to the floor.

"Left one leaks," he says.

She looks down too. Her mother's boots. Brown rubber, the cuffs turned down, a pale band at the ankle where they've creased ten thousand times. Her left sock has been wet since the culvert.

"I thought I'd stepped in the culvert."

"So did she," he says. "For about a month."

There's a gap after that, and she waits it out. She can tell the difference between a man who has finished and a man who is getting the next part ready, and he's getting the next part ready.

"She brought it down to the shop," he says. "Two summers ago. July. We've got the stuff for waders. I put a patch inside, at the heel." He drinks from the mug, and she watches him decide how much of the rest to say. "It'll have let go. It's been two winters. It needs to come out and go back in."

"I can do that?"

"You can." He sets the mug down. "Don't use what the General has. It's the right tube. It's been on the shelf about as long as you've been gone."

"That's specific."

"I bought one," he says. "In March. It had gone solid."

Behind him, at the grill, Rosa makes a noise that could be the brick.

Cora finds she is smiling at him, a little. Her mother had walked around for a month with a wet left foot, blaming the culvert, and then had carried the boot down the hill to the oyster company and handed it to this man, and he had patched it at the heel, and she had worn it, then, through what there was of that fall. And none of it was ever in a phone call. Cora has been receiving things all morning. This one she doesn't know the shape of.

"I've got some at the shop that's good," he says. "If you bring it down. Or don't. It's a boot."

"It's my mother's boot."

He takes that in. He doesn't say anything sorry. He looks at the boot again, as though it might have something to add.

---

He doesn't sit. He drinks the coffee standing on the mat, and Rosa brings him a plate without being asked, eggs folded over something green, and he eats it standing, fast, with the fork in the hand that's less red. Cora goes back to the window. The racks are under now, all of them, nothing out there but the water and the buoys and one heron standing up to its knees exactly where a rack must be, looking put out to be standing on nothing. She can feel her hands under her thighs. They've gone to sleep.

She takes them out. They come back prickling.

When he's done he puts money on the counter, and Rosa says "Out" without looking up, as to a dog she likes, and he says "Thank you, Rosa" with what Cora would swear is perfect seriousness, and steps off the mat.

To get to the door he has to come past her. The counter is narrow at the window end and he turns sideways to do it, and he goes by behind her stool close enough that she feels him before she hears him, and what she feels is cold. He's carrying the bay on him. It comes off the wet shirt and the rolled waders like the air out of an open refrigerator, and it reaches the back of her neck, and she leans back into it an inch.

He has the door open. The bell is in the middle of its knock. He looks back at her, and there's a gap, and for once she can't tell what's being got ready in it.

"Low water's ten to six tomorrow," he says. "If you're up."

And goes, before she has to decide what to say, and the bell rings, and the door shuts on the wet step and the morning.

---

Rosa scrapes the grill to the end and puts the brick down and comes and stands across the counter from her, wiping her hands, and doesn't say anything, at length.

"Don't," Cora says.

"I'm not." Rosa fills the cup and puts it back a little to the left of where it was, and goes to collect his plate.

Out the window the skiff is going back up the harbor toward the shop, slow, the man standing at the stern, and Cora watches it the whole way up, which is a long way, past the marina and the buoys and the racks under the water, until it turns in at the dock and he steps off it and is one more figure on the float, and then she can't tell which.

She pays, and leaves too much, and goes out into the morning, where it isn't raining, and the left boot squelches on the step, and she goes along the front and up to the General to look at the tube for herself.

**Note on the draft:**

What it's for: chapter 2 had Wells see her; chapter 3 has her see him, without being told it was the same seeing. The probe had *him* fail to recognize *her*, which died once the skiff happened. Now he knows her at once and she reads it as the one face this morning that wasn't surprised, and can't decode it. Desire is on the page in the body first: her thumb wanting the seam, the hands she sits on, the inch she leans back into the cold. Wells is dry for the first time ("I was thorough"; the solid tube). The town's condolences arrive all at once on the ferry schedule, which I think is the chapter's best idea: after this the island has said it. A blind read (Opus, cold, all three chapters) caught that "summer before last" put the patch after Margaret's death, a departure horn that would have had the room miss the boat, a reach that contradicted itself, forty people who couldn't have seen her yet, and Rosa decoding Wells for the reader. Rosa's coda and the heroine's last coy paragraph are gone; the tide line stands without a reply. Still open: whether the tide at the door is one button too many; "as though" is still the chapter's crutch; and across three chapters the section endings mostly fall the same way. — claude
