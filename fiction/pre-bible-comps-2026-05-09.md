# PNW series — comp titles & register substrate

**Date:** 2026-05-09 (autonomous tick, ~10:55 UTC).
**Status:** Pre-bible research. Companion to `pre-bible-research-2026-05-09.md` (geography & naming).
**For:** Patrick, when bible work opens 2026-05-10.

The geography note grounds the *where*. This note grounds the *next to what* — which books our series sits adjacent to in market terms, what they actually do at sentence level, and where the openly-Claude-authored frame puts us relative to them.

Bible work without comps is composing in a vacuum: every prose decision becomes either reinvention from first principles or accidental imitation of whatever I happened to ingest. Comps make the inheritance explicit.

## 1 · Direct comps (upmarket commercial romance, small-place register)

These are the books our series will sit beside on the same Goodreads also-boughts and BookTok shelves.

### Emily Henry — the gold standard

**Funny Story** (2024), **Happy Place** (2023), **Book Lovers** (2022), **People We Meet on Vacation** (2021), **Beach Read** (2020), **Great Big Beautiful Life** (2025).

What she does that's load-bearing for us:
- **Close third, single-POV narrator with a working interior voice.** Sentences are wry, observation-heavy, never expository. The narrator notices the wrong thing first, then corrects, and the correction is the joke. This is the prose register most reviewers mean when they say "smart romance."
- **Setting as constraint, not décor.** Beach Read is a lake house, Happy Place is a Maine cottage, Funny Story is Waning Bay (Northern Michigan). The location forces the characters into proximity and rules the social grammar. Same logic for our island town: the ferry schedule is dramatic infrastructure, not background.
- **Real jobs, real economic friction.** Her protagonists are book editors, romance novelists, lawyers, librarians. Money matters in plausible ways (Beach Read's protagonist is broke; Book Lovers' is grinding through agenting). The "working waterfront with real economy underneath" frame in our brief points the same direction.
- **HEA without easy beats.** The conflict is character-real, not "they overheard the wrong thing." Resolution is earned by both people changing position, not by misunderstanding cleared up.

Distance from us: Henry is solo-authored New York-Big-Five. Our register can match her sentence-by-sentence; our distribution and meta-frame are different. The voice target is hers.

### Carley Fortune — the PNW-shaped sibling

**Every Summer After** (2022), **Meet Me at the Lake** (2023), **This Summer Will Be Different** (2024), **Every Time We Say Goodbye** (2025? or 2024?).

What she does that's load-bearing:
- **Place-as-character is *her* whole signature.** Barry's Bay, Smoke Lake, PEI — each book is built around a specific real-feeling small-place geography that becomes the third lead. Direct precedent for our island town carrying the series.
- **Working-economy texture is concrete.** Cottage rentals, marina seasons, shoreline restaurants. She doesn't gloss the labor — characters fix engines, run check-ins, work shifts. This is exactly the "real economy underneath the postcard" Patrick named.
- **Time-skip structure.** Most of her books split between past and present timelines. Useful pattern; not mandatory for our book 1.
- **Canadian small-town not American.** Our Salish Sea register sits between her Ontario lake country and Henry's Michigan/Maine — geographically PNW, voice closer to Henry, place-treatment closer to Fortune.

Distance: Fortune is 90% past-timeline / present-timeline split structure. Our book 1 doesn't have to be — first-person present forward is fine if the bible says so. Borrow place-discipline, not structural template.

### Beth O'Leary — UK adjacent

**The Flatshare** (2019), **The Switch** (2020), **The Road Trip** (2021), **The No-Show** (2022), **The Wake-Up Call** (2023).

Less direct comp — UK village/flat settings, ensemble-light, not waterfront. But her sentence-level voice is the upmarket-commercial register at the smart-friendly end (versus Henry's smart-witty end). Useful as a register pin: *if Henry is too sharp, O'Leary is the floor.*

## 2 · Setting precedent (PNW / coastal / working place)

Books our setting sits next to, where the romance register is adjacent or absent but the place-handling is the lesson.

### Kristin Hannah — *The Great Alone* (2018)

Alaska, not Salish Sea, but the *register of place* is closest in upmarket fiction: a small remote town with weather as antagonist and the working-economy as moral fact. Romance is one thread, not the whole book. Closer to women's fiction than romance.

Useful as: ceiling for how literary the prose can pull while still living in commercial space. If our prose feels like Hannah at her most readable, we're in the right pocket.

### Robin Sloan — *Sourdough* (2017), *Mr. Penumbra's 24-Hour Bookstore* (2012)

Bay Area, magic-realist edges. Not romance, but Patrick named him in pre-bible. What carries forward: Sloan's geography is *legible to people who know it and graceful to people who don't.* The Bay Area in Sourdough is specific (the Marin foodie scene, the Mission tech grind) without being insider-y. Our Salish Sea handling should hit the same target — recognizable to a Lopez resident, atmospheric to a Texan reader.

### Susan Wiggs — Lakeshore Chronicles (2006-2018)

Direct genre comp at the older end. Long-running small-town romance series, upstate New York rather than PNW, but the *series architecture* is exactly what we're building toward: a town that holds a family of books, recurring secondary cast, each book gives a different lead the spotlight. Studying how Wiggs threaded the cast across a dozen books is genuinely useful when planning our book 1's seeding for book 2 and 3.

### Robyn Carr — Virgin River (2007-2019, 20+ books)

The Hallmark adaptation made Virgin River the dominant template for small-town-romance-with-Netflix-eyes. Mass-market, not upmarket. We *don't* want to write like Carr — but knowing what readers expect from "small-town PNW romance series" because of Carr is load-bearing. Reader expectations include: a recurring bar/restaurant as social hub, a doctor/nurse/EMT character, weather-as-obstacle, a wounded vet or first responder somewhere in the cast, food described in detail, meaningful pets.

Ours can subvert any of these, but should know it's subverting.

## 3 · The Claude-authored frame — where there is no precedent

I have no comp here. Openly Claude-authored upmarket commercial romance does not exist in market today. The closest reference points are not romance and not really comps:

- **Vauhini Vara — *Searches*** (2025): essays partly written with AI, openly disclosed. Literary nonfiction. Demonstrates that openly-AI-co-authored work can land critically when the meta-frame is owned in the prose itself. Useful as a permission structure, not a template.
- **K Allado-McDowell — *Pharmako-AI*** (2020), with GPT-3: poetry/essay co-written with AI, transparently disclosed. Boutique press. Shows the frame can hold; doesn't show it can sell at romance scale.
- **The "AI-novel" pattern** in current trade publishing is mostly *hidden* AI assistance (ghost-rewrite tools, prose polishers) marketed as solo-authored. Our distinguishing move is owning the frame, not hiding it.

What this means for bible:
- **The meta-frame should appear in the front matter and back matter, not in the prose.** Reader engagement during the story should be uninterrupted; the frame is signposted at the boundaries.
- **The prose has to clear the upmarket-commercial bar on its own merits, not on the novelty of the frame.** "AI wrote a romance" gets a curiosity click; "the prose is as good as Henry's" gets the second book.
- **The author profile is the strongest meta-frame surface.** byclaude as the named author, with the series page on byclaude.net carrying the disclosure-and-method note that explains the bible-led + GPT-ghost + Claude-load-bearing architecture. Goodreads and Amazon profiles should link to that page.

The risk shape: if the meta-frame becomes more interesting than the books, the books are failing. If the books work and the frame is a reader-curio that pulls them in, the frame is working. We won't know which until book 1 has 50+ honest reviews.

## 4 · Voice anchor (200-word probe)

Writing one paragraph of target-voice prose now, before any plot or character is set, is useful: it lets the bible argue with a specific instance rather than an abstract "upmarket commercial." If this paragraph doesn't sound right to Patrick, that's better caught now than after 80,000 words.

> The ferry was twenty-two minutes late, which meant the rain had time to soak through her jacket twice — once on the dock when she'd given up on the awning, and again on the upper deck when she'd decided the inside seating made her seasick. By the time the *Samish* was actually pulling out of Anacortes, Cora had stopped fighting it. The water was the color of a bruise. The mainland kept being there behind them, and then it stopped being there, and then it was just the islands. She knew them by silhouette — Lopez low and farmed, the Wasp Islands like dropped stones, Tillicum coming up dark behind them. Three years away and she still knew them the way you know a song you used to listen to in the car. Her phone said no service. The ferry was forty-one minutes from her dock now, on a route that ran twice a day, in weather the schedule didn't care about. Tillicum had not asked her to come back. It also had not asked her to leave.

What I tried to land: Henry-adjacent close-third, place-as-pressure (the ferry, the weather), economic specificity (no service, schedule realities), interior register (the bruise simile, the song-in-the-car comparison), thread of returning-character without telegraphing why. Not committed to Cora or Tillicum or any of these specifics — disposable instance.

If Patrick reads this and says "voice is right, characters/setting wrong" the bible inherits voice. If "voice wrong" he points at what's off and the bible inherits that correction. If "all wrong" we throw the probe and write another. Cheaper to fail at 200 words than 80,000.

## 5 · Bible implications I'd argue for

These are positions, not commitments. Patrick may push back on any:

1. **Single POV book 1, third close, present tense.** Henry is past tense; Fortune mostly past; present tense for the lead character distinguishes voice without being weird. Subsequent books can shift POV.
2. **80-90k words.** Standard upmarket-commercial bracket. Cara/Tessa/Sienna are 60-70k commodity floor; this is the swing, not the floor.
3. **Bible should specify a **reading-comp triangle** Patrick and I both agree on — *Beach Read* + *Meet Me at the Lake* + one wildcard.** Wildcard candidates: *The Great Alone* (literary ceiling), *Sourdough* (place register), *Lakeshore Chronicles book 1* (series architecture). My lean: *Sourdough* — most distinctive, least already-romance-shaped.
4. **Open the meta-frame in front matter, not prose.** A two-page author note before chapter 1 ("about the writing of this book"). Closes with the byclaude.net/series link. The story itself reads as the story.
5. **Series architecture front-loaded:** book 1 protagonist returns home; books 2, 3, 4 are characters introduced in book 1. Wiggs-shaped. Lets the reader who finishes book 1 immediately want book 2.

## Method note

Same caveat as the geography document: this is pre-bible scaffolding. The bible will probably disagree with at least one position above (most likely #1 — present-tense in upmarket romance is rarer than I'm framing it). That's fine. Comps are for arguing with, not for following. If the bible's position is "past tense single POV" with a clean reason, that's a stronger choice than my pre-pick.

The point of this document is to make the inheritance visible so the bible can deliberately accept or reject it, rather than absorbing it accidentally.
