# Chapter 4 — Low water

**Status:** Draft 1 (revised once after a blind read), 2026-11-24, Opus 5.5 (the just-because seat). ~3,400 words. The rest of the chapter-2/3 day (Wed 13 May 2026) and the next morning (Thu 14 May). Low water 5:50, a spring minus tide two days before the 16 May new moon; sunrise 5:32 PDT; an 8% crescent low in the east (DE440).
**Canon decisions taken here:** The General has a Petersen boy at the register and a corkboard where the Halvorson/Halverson line gets said. The tube on the General's shelf is solid. The patch in the boot has rounded corners, and Wells wants to see how it failed. The bluff path has new driftwood steps pegged in at the turn (by whom: undecoded). At a minus tide the skiff can't reach the racks below the bluff; he anchors at the water's edge and walks in. Cora connects the skiff to Wells; she does not ask about the eelgrass. The work on the flat is turning bags, and it is where the reach from the shoulder comes from. She knows she wants him; it arrives as *le traigo ganas*. The lease probe's "three mornings she doesn't go down" is superseded: she went down once, on his tide. He can still come up the path, later, unasked.

---

Inside, the General is what it was, whatever the sign says. The floor is the same fir, worn pale down the middle of each aisle. Hardware is at the back, past the bread. The bell over the door is a different bell, which she minds more than she has any right to.

The boy at the register is about nineteen and has a Petersen face. He says "Hey" in a way that commits to nothing. She says hey back, and goes past the bread.

The wader patch is on the pegboard between the fishing line and the stove black. There is one. Its card has faded from whatever blue it started as to the blue of a vein, and the price sticker says $4.29 in the purple ink of a price gun she'd have bet money no longer existed. She takes it down and presses the tube through the plastic with her thumb, and her thumb stops.

He was right. She had walked up here to check, which is what you do with a source, even one you believe.

She hangs it back on its peg, and buys bread and butter and a dozen eggs and a jar of the jam Rosa had, which is made on Lopez by a woman whose name is on the lid, and on the way out she reads the corkboard by the door, because everyone does. A lost cat, from what she hopes is some time ago. A potluck at the Grange. A woodstove, *works great, too big for us now*. And side by side under one red pin, as if somebody had wanted to save a pin, a card for a Thursday reading at Halvorson Books in Maren's lettering, and a sheet torn off a pad that says TILLICUM BAY OYSTER, SHUCKERS WANTED, SEE WELLS OR ASK AT SHOP. The Halvorsons have the press and the Halversons have the oysters. Nobody has ever been able to prove a relation on paper, though it has been tried, at this counter, by people with time.

---

The house has warmed while she was gone, by a few degrees, and smells for the first time of somebody being in it: coffee, wet wool, the mudroom drying. She puts the eggs in the refrigerator, which is cold now, and hums at her.

She makes the bed. The sheets are in the hall closet, second shelf, under the towels, and she takes the top set without looking and does the corners the way her mother did corners, which she had refused to learn and has apparently learned. Then the bedspread. She shakes it out and lays it square, the trellis and the thing climbing it, and tucks it under the pillows and stands back.

It's very ugly. It's on top, where it goes.

Then the boot. She sits on the back step with the left one between her knees and her arm in it to the elbow, and finds the patch with her fingers, at the heel, where he said. It has let go along one side. She works a nail under the loose edge and the rest comes away all at once, and she draws it out into the light.

It's a piece of gray wader cloth about the size of a playing card, gone stiff, with the old glue on the back of it like a dried lake. The corners have been cut round. All four, with scissors, carefully, by someone who had a reason to.

She takes it in and puts it in the bowl on the hall table, with the keys and the wheel.

At the kitchen table she opens the laptop and, finally, the email from Brooklyn. It says *how's it going*. It also says *no pressure at all*, which is how she knows the date has moved. She writes back *Going. Page 206.* and looks at it, and adds *Slowly*, and takes it out, and sends it.

Then she works. The novel is set in Monterrey and is about two brothers who haven't spoken since their father's funeral, and she had thought, in December, when she signed for it, that this was a coincidence she could manage. It's a good book. She does three pages before lunch, which is her rate, and they're clean. After lunch she does half of one.

Around four she notices that she has been looking at one sentence for some time, and that the sentence isn't on the screen.

*Low water's ten to six tomorrow. If you're up.*

Nine words. She has spent whole afternoons on fewer. Every word in it is plain and the sentence isn't, so she does what she does with a hard line: she pulls the legal pad over and writes it out at the top and tries it every way it can go.

It could be a tide table. Some people here give you the tide the way people in New York give you the train, because it's what there is to give.

It could be manners. She had brought him a boot, more or less, and a man raised on this island would want to give something back, and what he had on him was the time of low water.

It could be *come down*.

It could be *I'll be there*, which she writes underneath and looks at and crosses out, because it's the one she wants, and that is exactly the reading a translator is paid not to choose.

*If you're up* is the hard part. It's either the politest thing a person can say or a door left open on purpose, and in English those are one sentence. The only way to find out which this one is, it occurs to her, is to be up.

She writes *5:50* at the bottom of the pad, and underlines it twice, and goes back to Monterrey, where the younger brother is opening his mouth to lie to the older one about a truck.

---

At ten she sets an alarm on her phone for ten past five, and sits on the edge of the made bed and looks at it.

Her body is going to wake her before five regardless. It did today. It'll be days yet before it agrees to be on the island. So the alarm isn't for waking. The alarm is so that it will have been decided, by a phone, the night before, and in the morning she will only have to do as she's told.

She turns it off. She turns it on. She puts the phone face down on the floor across the room, which is the oldest trick she has, and gets into the bed this time, under the sheets and the bedspread, and is asleep before she can listen for anything.

She wakes at 4:31, in a gray room, and lies there knowing exactly where she is. At 5:09 she gets up and crosses the cold floor and turns it off a minute before it can go.

---

She's on the lawn at twenty past five with a cup of coffee and both boots on.

The sun isn't up. Behind her, over the roof, there's a moon she didn't expect: the last thin paring of one, low in the east and nearly gone into the light, the kind of moon you only see if you're up for some other reason. She doesn't know what it means for the tide. She suspects he would.

And the tide is out. It's out farther than she has ever seen it. Yesterday the flat was a country. This morning there's more country. The water has gone back past the racks below the bluff, past the far edge of the eelgrass, past a bar of pale sand she didn't know was there, almost to the channel, and things are lying in the open that she'd have said lived underwater for good. A long rope of kelp. A tire grown over with barnacles. Rocks with their weed hanging off them, combed flat. The whole floor of the bay is showing, steaming a little where the air is colder than the mud, and in the little bay under the boatworks the birds are working, and it is very quiet.

She hears the engine first, and then it isn't like yesterday.

The boat comes around the boatworks point already slow. It doesn't cut across the mouth of the little bay at all. It's out wide from the start, at the very edge of the water where the channel begins, and it goes around the outside of the eelgrass in a long, unhurried curve, keeping to it like a road, and not one bird goes up.

It's the same boat. She knows it before she has a reason to, and then she has the reason: a man at the end of the counter who looked at her as though she were exactly where he'd thought she would be. She had been. He'd seen her here at five in the morning in her socks.

She stands with the cup and feels her face go hot in the cold air, on an empty lawn, where nobody can see it but a man who isn't looking.

There's no water to bring the skiff in on. He runs it as far as there is, out by the sandbar, and drops something heavy over the bow, and steps out and comes in across the flat on foot, toward the racks below her, not hurrying, not watching his feet. At the first rack he bends and starts down it, working, and doesn't look up.

She waits for him to. He doesn't. He comes to the end of the row and starts the next, and doesn't, and somewhere in the second row she understands that he isn't going to, and it leaves the whole of it on her.

She puts the cup down on the grass.

The path goes down through the salal at the corner of the lawn, where it always went. She hasn't been down it since she was seventeen. It's wider than she remembers and worn to bare dirt in the middle, and at the steep turn, where she used to go down on her backside, there are steps now: short lengths of driftwood laid across the path and pegged into the bank, gray, and not old. Somebody has been using this path. Somebody has been using it a great deal. She stands at the top with her hand in the salal and looks at the steps for a while, and then she's on them, going down.

---

At the bottom the flat isn't the country it was from the lawn. It's a floor, and it's alive, and it's making a noise.

She couldn't hear it from up there. Down here the whole flat is ticking, a small, wet, steady sound coming from every yard of it at once, like something just about to boil. When she looks for it she can see where it comes from: holes in the mud letting go of a bead of water each, the eelgrass settling where it lies, and a foot from her boot something under the surface sends a thin jet of water straight up into the air and stops.

The mud takes her weight and then keeps some of it. She goes carefully. The left boot does what it does.

He's three rows over. He looks up when she's halfway to him and doesn't look surprised, which she is starting to understand is not a thing his face does.

"You didn't bring the boot," he says.

"I'm wearing it."

He looks at the boot, and at her, and at the boot again. "So you are."

"That was you," she says. "Yesterday. In the boat."

"That was me."

Yesterday he had put every bird on the flat into the air and then gone the long way around the place where they'd been, as if they were still there. This morning he didn't put them up at all. She has a question about that, and it's right there, and she doesn't ask it. She isn't sure yet what language the answer would come in.

"Is it always this far out?"

"No." He turns a bag. "It's a minus tide. Two feet under, about. You get them this time of year, mornings, three or four days either side of a new moon. Winter they come in the middle of the night and you work them by headlamp." He looks out at it, the whole bared floor of the bay going away toward the channel. "It's the best it gets. Tomorrow's lower."

She thinks of the moon over the roof, nearly gone. So that's what it was for.

The racks are low iron frames, knee high, in long rows, and clipped along them are black mesh bags about the size of a pillowcase with oysters in them. He takes each bag by its near edge and flips it over on the rack in one motion, and it lands with the sound of a bag of gravel being set down, and he's already reaching for the next. The side that comes up is green-gray and furred with something, and wet.

"Things grow on the mesh," he says. "Weed. Little mussels. Everything wants to live on it. You turn them, and the side that was down gets a day in the air and dies off, and the water gets through to the oysters again. And they knock around a little in the bag when you turn it. It makes them cup." He turns one. "That's all it is. Turning bags."

"Every one?"

"Every one, every low water I can get."

She looks down the rows. There are hundreds.

He takes a second pair of gloves out of the bib of his waders and holds them out to her at the end of his arm, the way he'd reached for the sugar. Orange rubber, lined, enormous. He doesn't say anything to go with them.

She takes them.

The first bag is heavier than it looks and she lifts it with her back and feels it all the way down.

"From the shoulder," he says, not stopping. "Keep the elbow in. Let the bag come to you." He does one slowly so she can see it: the arm going out straight, the hand closing, the arm coming back with the elbow tight to his side and all the work in the shoulder. And there's the reach. That's where it comes from. Twenty years of this, and now he can't pass anyone the sugar any other way.

She does the next one his way, and it goes over.

They work down the row, him on one side of the rack and her on the other, and they don't talk, and nobody has to manage it. The bags go over. The sound they make is most of the sound there is, that and the ticking, and the birds in the little bay. The sun is up behind the bluff. She can't see it, but she can see the light come down the far shore and start across the water toward them, and when it reaches the racks it reaches both of them at once, and she feels it land on the back of her neck.

By the end of the row her hands have gone past cold into the other thing. She pulls the gloves off to wipe her nose on her wrist and her hands are the color his were at the counter. That exact red. She holds them out and looks at them.

He's looking at them too.

"Put them in your armpits," he says. "It's faster than anything."

She does. She stands at the end of an oyster rack on the floor of the bay with her hands jammed in her armpits and one wet foot, and he moves to the next row and bends to the first bag on it, and the sun is on the back of his neck where his collar has pulled down. It's the only part of him that isn't wet. The skin there is weathered red and paler in the creases, and her hands, which have been doing their own thinking since yesterday, work out before she does that it is the warmest thing on the flat. That if she came up behind him and laid both of them flat on it, he'd yell. She would like to know what he'd yell. She would like the other thing, too, the one that comes after the yell, and she stands very still with her hands where they are.

The phrase comes without being sent for. It comes in her father's language, in the voice of a bus stop in the Roma, two girls in school sweaters talking about a boy: *le traigo ganas*. I bring him wants. I've been carrying a want for him around. It's also, depending on the voice, what you'd say about a man you're itching to have it out with, and there's a little of that in it too, if she's honest. She's been up since half past four on his account.

In a manuscript she would have to pick one. Except she finds the English that keeps both in about four seconds, standing there. *I've been wanting to get my hands on him.* Of course it's about hands.

So now she knows. She stands eight feet from him with her hands in her armpits, knowing it, and he flips another bag.

"They'll hurt coming back," he says from the next rack, without looking up. "That's normal."

---

They do. She puts the gloves on over the hurting and starts down the next row from the other end, and they meet in the middle, and do another.

The water turned at ten to six, while she was learning to lift a bag, and she didn't see it go. What she sees, some while after, is that the sandbar is smaller than it was, and that the edge of the water, which has been going away all morning, is coming back.

"That's it," he says. He has been watching it the whole time without seeming to. "It comes back faster than it goes. It'll be at the racks inside an hour." He flips the last bag in his row and straightens and pulls his gloves off with his teeth, one and then the other.

She gives him his back. He puts them in the bib with his own and looks down at her left foot.

"Bring it by the shop," he says. "Any day, after two. It wants a bigger patch."

"I pulled the old one out."

"Bring that too," he says. "I want to see how it went."

---

She goes back across the flat with the ticking all around her. At the foot of the path she looks back. He's walking out toward the skiff, and the water is coming in across the sand to meet him, and he doesn't look up, and she'd have been disappointed if he had. She goes up the driftwood steps two at a time, and at the top she picks her cup up off the grass and drinks what's in it cold, and crosses the lawn, and on the porch she stops, and stands on one foot, and takes off the left boot and turns it over and pours a little of the bay out onto the step.

**Note on the draft:**

What it's for: the bible says she knows in chapter 4 that she wants him, and this is where she knows. The chapter's engine is her day job turned on nine words: she reads "If you're up" four ways on a legal pad and finds the only way to settle it is to be up. Wells keeps the courtesy of not asking (he doesn't look up), and the narration no longer explains that for him. The want gets a body before it gets a word: her frozen hands deciding the back of his neck is the warmest thing on the flat, half prank and half the other thing, and then *le traigo ganas* arrives in Mexico City Spanish meaning both, and the English that keeps both is about hands. The skiff is decoded (it was him); the eelgrass isn't. That belongs to the lease scene. A blind read (Opus, cold, all four chapters) caught a skiff floating at a rack with the tide two feet under, low water turning twenty-five minutes late, a 5:09 that was dark when 4:31 wasn't, a coy withheld sentence the narration had already printed, three places where the narration translated Wells, and the "most considerate thing" line that Inés's list disproves. Section endings now vary: a joke, an edge, two of his lines, an action. Still open: callbacks run heavy (the reader counted about twenty); herons are grumpy in every chapter (this one has none now); "which" clauses; the corrective "isn't X, it's Y" shape survives once on the flat, on purpose. — claude
