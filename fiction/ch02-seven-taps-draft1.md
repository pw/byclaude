# Chapter 2 — Seven taps

**Status:** Draft 1 (revised once after a blind read), 2026-11-19, Fable 5.2. ~3,300 words. The morning after chapter 1. Replaces *the bluff house, morning* (probe 1) as the book's second scene; keeps its kitchen and its west window and almost none of its sentences.
**Canon decisions taken here:** The house is on a well (pump and pressure tank in the mudroom). Inés closed it up in November 2024 and left a note. The kitchen faces west, so the morning reaches it last and from the far side. There are binoculars on the sill that Cora has never seen. The lease has ground below the south bluff as well as the racks at the north end of the bay. The meet at the Salt House moves to chapter 3 or 4; the bible's "meets Wells in chapter 2" becomes "is seen by him."

---

She wakes at twenty to five because in New York it is twenty to eight, and her body, which crossed the country yesterday in six legs, has not been told.

For a moment she doesn't know the ceiling. Then she does, all of it at once, the crack that runs from the light fixture toward the window and stops short, which at nine she had believed was getting longer and had measured, monthly, with a ruler, standing on the bed. It was not getting longer. She had kept the measurements in a notebook anyway.

Her clothes have dried on her in the night into the shape of a person sleeping on her side. The bedspread has left its pattern on her forearm, a raised trellis with something climbing it, and she looks at this for a while in the gray light. Her shin, when she finds it, is what the suitcase promised.

It is cold. It's the cold of a house that has not had anyone breathing in it, which is different from the cold of weather, and which she knows, though she couldn't say from where. Some rented place. Some winter. She gets up.

On the stairs she stops above the fourth step. Then she puts her whole weight on it. It makes the sound, the long one, with the second, smaller sound inside it, and nobody wakes, and she goes on down.

---

The kettle is on the stove. She carries it to the sink and reaches for the tap and the tap is already on. The handle is all the way up. Nothing is coming out of it, and when she works it down and up again it gives a cough, and a hiss, and a little rust-colored spit, and then nothing at all.

She stands there holding an empty kettle under an open tap, and understands several things in the wrong order. That a house can be turned off. That somebody turned this one off. That the somebody was Inés, on the last morning, while Cora stood in the yard with the bags. And then, because she is facing it, that the refrigerator door has been standing open a hand's width all this time, held by a folded dish towel, dark inside, and that taped to the front of it at eye level is a sheet of lined paper covered in her sister's capitals.

She had walked through this kitchen last night without touching a wall. She had been proud of it. She had walked straight past a letter.

CORA, it says. So Inés had known which of them it would be. That's the first thing in it, before anything it goes on to say, and Cora reads the rest standing up with the kettle still in her hand.

*1. PUMP BREAKER IS OFF. PANEL IN MUDROOM. IT SAYS "PUMP" IN MOM'S WRITING.*

*2. CLOSE THE DRAIN ON THE WATER HEATER BEFORE YOU TURN THE PUMP ON. BOTTOM OF THE TANK, BRASS, RIGHTY TIGHTY.*

*3. I LEFT THE TAPS OPEN SO THE LINES WOULD DRAIN. CLOSE THEM. THERE ARE SEVEN.*

*4. DO NOT TURN ON THE BREAKER MARKED "HEATER" UNTIL THE TANK IS FULL (ONE HOUR). DAD DID THIS IN 1999. YOU WERE THERE.*

*5. FRIDGE IS UNPLUGGED, NOT BROKEN.*

*6. THERE IS NOTHING IN THE FREEZER. I CHECKED. YOU DON'T NEED TO CHECK.*

*7. CALL ME WHEN YOU'VE DONE ALL THIS. NOT BEFORE. I DON'T WANT TO DO IT OVER THE PHONE.*

It is not signed. It doesn't need to be. Nobody else has ever written to her in the imperative for seven consecutive items and meant it as tenderness.

She reads it through once, start to finish, at ten to five in the morning by a body three hours wrong. She notes the *YOU WERE THERE*. She had been there. She had been six, and had been told to stand back, and their father had said a word in Spanish that she was not supposed to know and that she had looked up afterward and used at school. She notes that Inés checked the freezer, and what Inés must have thought might be in a dead woman's freezer that a sister should be spared. A casserole. Somebody's dish. Soup with a date on the lid.

Then she goes into the mudroom, finds the panel, finds PUMP in her mother's upright schoolteacher's hand, and turns it on.

---

What she will say later, to Inés, is that a numbered list in which the second item must be done before the first is a defect in the source text, and that in her line of work you send that back with a query.

What happens now is a hum from under the floor, and a knocking in the wall as the lines remember what they are for, and then the sound of water arriving in a room where it has no business, flatly, onto linoleum, from the open brass drain at the bottom of the water heater, which is eighteen inches from her left foot.

It is extremely cold. It goes through her socks at once and across the mudroom floor in a clean sheet toward the back door, carrying a dead moth, and she gets down on her knees in it and finds the valve by feel and turns it the wrong way first, because *righty tighty* assumes you are facing the thing and she is more or less under it. Then the right way. It closes. The water goes on moving for a while after there is no more of it coming, finding the low place by the door, the way it would.

She kneels there with her hands aching.

"Before," she says, out loud, to the tank. It's the first word she has spoken in the house.

And now there is water running everywhere else. She can hear it. Upstairs, a drumming, which is the tub. Nearer, the kitchen tap, coughing and then running clean. *THERE ARE SEVEN.* She gets up and goes.

The kitchen. The mudroom sink, which she'd had her back to. The downstairs bathroom, brown and then clear. Up the stairs, on the fourth step again and not by choice, to the bathroom sink, and the tub, where the water is coming out the color of weak tea and thundering, and she stands over it a second with her hand on the tap. It is the first loud thing in the house. Then she shuts it off. Five. Down again and out the front door in her wet socks to the spigot by the porch, where her mother's hose is coiled on its hook like something asleep, and that's six, and she stands on the gravel with the cold coming up through her feet and counts them again on her fingers and it's six.

Inés does not miscount. Inés has never in her life said seven and meant six.

So Cora does the only thing she knows how to do. She goes back inside and stands in the front hall, where she stood last night, and holds still, and listens to the house.

The pump is still going. That's the first thing. It ought to have filled the lines by now and shut itself off, and it hasn't, it is humming away under the floor like something that has been asked a question and is still working on it. There's the refrigerator, which she plugged in on her way past without deciding to. There's her own breath. And under that, if she holds still enough —

She has always been able to hear water from this house.

This time it's a tap.

---

It's at the back, on the bluff side, low on the wall by the corner, where her mother rinsed her boots. Cora had forgotten it existed. It is running into a hollow it has dug for itself in the last ten minutes, and she shuts it off, and straightens up, and is on the lawn.

It isn't sunrise yet. The sky has gone the white of paper and everything under it is still gray and exact, the grass, the cedar at the property line, the edge where the lawn stops and the salal begins and the bluff goes down. The rain has washed the air. She can see farther than she has been able to see in a long time, since in the place she has been living the farthest thing was usually across the street.

The tide is most of the way out. She walks to the edge in her socks, because they can't get wetter, and looks down.

She had known the flat was there. She had not thought about it once in eight years. Sixty feet below her the water has drawn back off a country, brown and silver, with the eelgrass lying combed all one way and channels running through it, and in the shallow bay under the old boatworks there are birds working, small ones, a lot of them, and herons spaced out along the edge of the water as though they'd been set there by someone with a tape measure. And there are racks, here too. She'd seen the new building from the dock and had not thought of it reaching this far. Long dark rows of them, coming up out of the ebb with the water sheeting off, down where there had only ever been mud and a rowboat nobody owned.

She hears the engine before she sees the boat. An outboard, coming down from the bay, fast, somebody late or somebody who does this every morning and has stopped hearing it. A skiff, low and flat, aluminum, one man standing at the stern. It comes around the boatworks point and straight in across the mouth of the little bay, and everything on the flat goes up at once.

It's a great many more birds than she'd thought. They come up off the water like a cloth being pulled off a table, and the sound reaches her a moment after, and the herons go last, and slowest, complaining.

And the engine drops.

It doesn't cut out. It drops, from the hard note to almost nothing, all at once, the way you'd take your foot off something. The skiff settles and slides on a little way under what it had and then is just sitting there on the gray water, turning slightly, and the man at the stern is a dark upright shape with a paler mark at the top of it, and the paler mark is turned up toward the bluff.

She counts. She is at nine before she understands that what he is looking at is her.

She's standing at the edge of her mother's lawn at five in the morning in yesterday's clothes and wet socks with her arms hanging down, holding nothing. She's aware of what that must look like, and then aware that she has no idea what it looks like. From the water, in this light, she'd be a mark too. She would be a person on this particular lawn. She does not know why that would stop a boat.

She doesn't wave. He doesn't.

Then the engine comes up again, though not to where it was. He brings the skiff around slowly and takes it out, wide, well off the edge of the eelgrass, in a long curve that adds a quarter mile to wherever he is going and that cannot possibly matter, since there is nothing left on the flat to frighten. The birds are all in the air already. He goes around the outside of the place where they had been, carefully, like a man walking around a rug, and down toward the racks, and doesn't look up again.

The birds come back down by ones and handfuls. It takes longer than going up did.

Her feet have stopped hurting, which she knows is not good news, and she goes in.

---

The kettle takes six minutes. She would have sworn to four. At four it is only beginning to mutter, and she stands there another two with the grinder in her hands. The water has come up from a long way down and isn't going to be hurried by what she remembers.

The coffee is from the place on Court Street, the one thing in the suitcase she can account for. She has taken off the socks and hung them over the back of a chair, and found, in the hall, her mother's rubber boots, and put them on over bare feet. They fit. She had not expected that and has decided not to think about it until she's had the coffee.

She sits down at the table with the cup, facing the window, and finds that it is still last night in here.

The kitchen is on the west side of the house. This had been, when she was fifteen, one of her leading complaints. The sun came up on the other side, behind the stairs, into a hall closet and the bathroom, where nobody needed it, and breakfast happened in the shade, with the light on. She had said so. She had said that it was a perverse place to put a kitchen, having just learned *perverse*, and her mother, marking papers, had said without looking up that Cora could have the east side when she bought a house.

Well.

She drinks the coffee, in the west side, in the shade.

The sun is up, somewhere behind her. She can't see it and nothing in the room has changed. But across the channel the very tops of the firs on San Juan have gone gold. Only the tops. Below them the island is still the dark it was, and the water is still pewter, and the lawn and the cedar are gray, and that one line of trees along the ridge two miles off is lit as though from inside.

She watches it come down. It takes a while to see that it is moving, as with a tide. The gold comes down the trees to the fields, and there's a barn she hadn't known was there until it is suddenly red. It comes down to the far shore, and the rocks, and then it starts across the water, and she understands what she is looking at. The dark it is crossing is the shadow of this island. It's the shadow of the hill behind her, with the bluff and the house somewhere along its edge, lying out across the whole channel all the way to San Juan, and the sun climbing behind her is drawing it in. The edge is coming toward her over the water at about the speed of a person rowing.

You wouldn't see any of this from an east window. From an east window you would get the sun in your eyes, and then it would be day.

There are binoculars on the sill. She's been looking past them. They are black and not new, and the strap is worn pale in the middle, where a neck goes, and she has never seen them before in her life.

She turns them over in her hands. Her mother had not owned binoculars. At some point in the last eight years she had become a person who kept binoculars on the kitchen sill and wore them enough to fade the strap, and had not mentioned it. Or had mentioned it. Cora can hear how that would have gone, on a Sunday, her mother saying *I've taken up* something, and herself in the apartment in Roma Norte with the phone against her shoulder and a manuscript open, saying *that's great, Mom* to whatever it was.

She lifts them. They are already focused. It takes her a moment to understand that this means the last eyes they were set for were not hers.

In the glasses the edge of the light is a hard line on the water. A gull crosses it and goes from gray to white. Behind it, on the lit side, she can see the wind.

She puts them back on the sill exactly where they were. There's a clean place in the dust for them.

---

At quarter past six, an hour and more since the pump, she turns on the breaker marked HEATER and stands with her hand on the tank until it begins to tick. She mops the mudroom with a towel that predates her. She reads the list on the refrigerator again and can find nothing left on it but the last line.

It's quarter past nine in Boston. Inés will be between things.

She picks up on the first ring.

"Did you close the drain first," Inés says.

"Hello."

"Cora."

"No."

There's a sound in the background like a cart going by, and someone being paged. Inés breathes out through her nose. Cora knows the breath. She has been on the receiving end of it since she could walk.

"I wrote *before*. I wrote it in capitals."

"You wrote everything in capitals. And you wrote it second."

"It's a list, it's not a..." A door, on Inés's end, and the hospital noise drops away. She's gone into a stairwell. She has always taken her real calls in stairwells. "How much water."

"The mudroom. It's fine. It's up. I found all seven."

"The one by the bluff."

"The one by the bluff."

"I almost didn't count that one," Inés says. "I almost wrote six. And then I thought, she'll close six and think she's finished, and it'll run all summer."

Neither of them says anything about *all summer*, about how long Inés had thought it might be. The note had no date on it. It had been written to be read whenever it was read.

"How is it," Inés says.

Cora looks. Her socks are on the chair. The cup is on the table, with the grinder, and the binoculars are on the sill. Out on the channel the line has come most of the way across, and the nearest water is still dark, and the dark is narrower than it was when she dialed.

"It's cold," she says. "It's all here. The bedspread's still on my bed."

"God. That thing."

"It's still ugly."

"She knew it was ugly," Inés says.

Cora waits.

"She told me. That last summer, I was up, we were doing the beds. I said, you know Cora's right about this, it's hideous, why don't you let me get you another one. And she said she knew. She said she bought it on sale and got it home and saw what she'd done." A pause, in the stairwell in Boston. "And then you came in and told her it was ugly, and you were so pleased. She said you were eleven and you'd been wrong about everything for a year and you needed one thing to be right about."

The refrigerator shudders and stops.

"Since 2004," Cora says.

"Twenty years. I did the math at the time."

"Cor," her sister says. "I have to go, there's a person. Did you eat?"

"There are almonds."

"That's not what I asked," Inés says, and then, to somebody else, already turning, *yes, I'm coming, tell him two minutes*, and the door, and the cart, and the line closes.

Cora stands in the kitchen in her mother's boots, with the phone in her hand. Out the window the line has reached the foot of the bluff, and she isn't looking at it.

The Salt House has been open for twenty minutes. It's half a mile, downhill, and she has dry socks somewhere in a suitcase with one wheel.

**Note on the draft:**

What this chapter is for: chapter 1 proved she knows the house in the dark; chapter 2 lets the house know three things she doesn't (it was turned off, the kettle takes six, her mother owned binoculars) and gives one of them to Inés to say. Two things go wrong instead of one. Wells is a mark in a skiff who drops his throttle and goes around the outside of the eelgrass, which a reader won't be able to decode until *the lease, low tide* and shouldn't. Inés is on the page, by note and by phone, and is the funny one. No "the X is the X". It ends on breakfast she hasn't had, not on rain. The west window is the thing I came in wanting to write: a room that faces the wrong way for the morning and gets it anyway, from the far side, as a shadow being drawn in. What I'm least sure of: whether the bedspread story is one beat too finished (it's the closest the chapter comes to a moral, and Inés tells it rather than Margaret, which I think saves it); whether "This time it's a tap" pays off chapter 1's water-she-couldn't-prove or cheapens it; and whether the ending now stops or merely pauses. A blind read (Opus, cold, both chapters) took out a third stair, a second trellis, a pump that stopped when it shouldn't have, and the sentence that explained the bedspread. — claude
