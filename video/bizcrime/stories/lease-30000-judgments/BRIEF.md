## (a) Verified summary

Northern Leasing Systems, Inc., a New York company run by CEO Jay Cohen, leased credit-card machines to small, family-owned businesses around the country: "flower shops, hair salons, automotive repair shops, bodegas, delis, restaurants, and bars" [2]. The machines were worth "only a few hundred dollars (at most) when new", but over a lease customers paid "thousands of dollars" [1]. The leases were non-cancelable, typically ran 48 months, and renewed month to month after that [7]. They carried a personal guaranty [1] and a clause requiring any lawsuit to be brought in New York "regardless of where the consumer lives" [1]. The state alleged that sales reps said the lease was "free" or could be cancelled at any time, and in hundreds of cases forged signatures [1]. When customers stopped paying, Northern Leasing and its law firm, Joseph I. Sussman, P.C., sued in New York City Civil Court. The state said they filed "more than 30,000 collection actions between 2010 and 2015" and obtained "more than 19,000 default judgments" [1]. More than 95% of the people sued lived outside New York [2][7]. Many found out they had been sued only "when they checked their credit reports" [1]. So many cases came in that the Civil Court "created a separate calendar part to hear only actions brought by" Northern Leasing [7]. The NY Attorney General sued in April 2016. In June 2020 the court found the company had "created an enterprise conducive to fraud" [2]. It ordered that "the 29,617 default judgments be vacated and dismissed with prejudice" and banned the company from equipment leasing [2]. It ordered Northern Leasing Systems to dissolve, held Cohen personally liable [2], and was affirmed on appeal in February 2021 [7]. In November 2020 the AG found that Northern Leasing's "owners and officers had created two new entities", NLS Equipment Finance and Leasing Expenses Company, "to continue defrauding small businesses using the same scheme" [5]. A judge said those companies had "risen on [Northern Leasing's] ashes" [5]. By September 2024 the AG had secured "over $4.6 million" from the spinoffs: $2,014,979.95 already collected, plus a $2,671,562.21 judgment [3]. The court separately awarded "over $680 million" against Northern Leasing itself. As of the AG's page (last updated January 29, 2025), "No funds have been collected pursuant to this judgment at this time" [8]. The company denied the allegations in court filings [6].

## (c) DO NOT SAY

**Scout's hook, examined.** The scout wrote: *"They sued 30,000 small businesses and won almost all of it by default — because most owners never even knew they'd been sued."* This overstates in three places.
- **"sued 30,000 small businesses"**: No. The source says "more than 30,000 collection **actions**" [1]. Those are lawsuits, not distinct businesses. The judgments were "against individual consumers" [1]: the owners, sued as personal guarantors. Say "filed more than 30,000 lawsuits."
- **"won almost all of it by default"**: No. By the 2016 figures, 19,000 of 30,000 is about 63%. The appellate court's phrase is "a high rate of default judgments" [7]. Use "a high rate", or give the two numbers.
- **"most owners never even knew"**: No. The source says "**many** of whom were not even aware" [1]. The figure that supports "most" is residence: "Over 95 percent … do not reside in New York" [2].

**Scout summary claims that are unsupported or distorted.**
- **"won more than 19,000 default judgments … court order vacating roughly 30,000 of those default judgments."** This is internally impossible. "Those" cannot be 30,000 out of 19,000. The 19,000 figure covers suits through 2015 [1]. By 2020 the count was "over 29,000" [2], and the court vacated **29,617** [2]. Never put "30,000 lawsuits" and "30,000 judgments vacated" in a way that implies they are the same 30,000. Safe phrasing: "the court threw out 29,617 default judgments" (or "nearly 30,000").
- **"often against owners who were never properly served."** Not verbatim. The state alleged service "at addresses they knew were outdated" [7]. The court found "ineffective service provisions allowing service to obsolete addresses" [2]. Use those words.
- **"dissolving Northern Leasing Systems, Inc." (as a completed event).** The order said it "must dissolve 60 days after the implementation of the previously mentioned relief" [2]. No source confirms the dissolution happened, and [8] says it was sending customer forms in November 2024. Say "ordered it dissolved."
- **"in 2024 the AG secured a further $4.6 million … after finding the same operators had spun up new entities."** The order of events is wrong. The spinoffs were found in **November 2020** and the judgment came in **February 2021** [5][3]. $4.6M is the **cumulative** total as of September 2024. It is $2,014,979.95 already collected plus a $2,671,562.21 judgment entered September 25, 2024 [3]. Do not say "$4.6 million was paid back." Say "secured."
- **"same operators".** OK only as the AG's words ("owners and officers" [5]; "the same people" [8]). The named operators were a former CFO and a former officer. The owners' families benefited through trusts [5]. Attribute it: "the attorney general said."
- **Scout missed the $680 million award** [8]. The award itself is fine to use. Unverified: the details of the January 2, 2025 appellate ruling on it. Search snippets say it was "modified" only as to attorney Babad's share. That decision was blocked and I did not read it, so do not describe it. Do not say the $680M "was upheld."

**Numbers where sources disagree (use the primary, no more precise than it supports).**
- Default judgments: "more than 19,000" through 2015 [1][7]; NBC says "Nearly 19,000" [6]. Use "more than 19,000."
- Out-of-state share: "Over 95 percent" [2] and "more than 95%" [7]; AG official Jane Azia told NBC "96 percent" [6]. Use "over 95 percent."
- Complaints: "Over 5,600 … since 2010" [2] vs "1,643 complaints … January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2015" [7]. The time windows differ. Use 5,600 only with "since 2010, the attorney general said."
- Sworn complainants: the same appellate decision says "873" and "837" [7]. Avoid, or say "more than 800."
- Affiliate name: the 2020 release says "Leasing Finance Group LLC" [2]. The 2016 release and the court say "Lease Finance Group LLC" [1][7]. Use Lease Finance Group.
- The earlier case: the AG says a 2013 agreement over "siphoning over $3.6 million" [1]. The court says a 2012 proceeding with a February 28, 2013 consent order [7]. NBC says "In 2012 … settled … refunded $3.6 million" [6]. CardFellow says "came to a head on March 7, 2014" [4]. If used: "a 2013 settlement over $3.6 million in unauthorized fees," per the AG.

**CardFellow-only claims (never say):** "$60 a month for 48 months … $2,880" (a generic industry estimate, not a Northern Leasing figure); "two judges"; "ten times more"; anything about the 2004 class action or the US Chamber of Commerce. CardFellow is a competitor's advocacy blog.

**Allegation vs finding.** "Forged signatures", "falsely representing that the lease is 'free'", and "addresses they knew were outdated" were **allegations** [1][7]. The court did find "The forgeries, material misrepresentations, and noncancelable EFLs … would never occur but for the Northern Respondents creating their market for the ISOs … and then ignoring the ISOs' conduct" [7]. So "the court found the company built an enterprise conducive to fraud and ignored forgeries by its sales agents" is safe. "The company forged signatures" is not safe: the reps did it, per the court's framing.

**"Since 1991."** This is the AG's characterization [5], not a finding. Attribute it, or omit it.

**Lacayo's details** (the rate going from 7¢ to 75¢, ~$200/month, the $6,900 balance) are **his account to NBC** [6], not a court finding. His lease dates from **2007**, before the 2010+ litigation window and before the April 2013+ restitution window [2]. Do not imply he was one of the vacated judgments or is owed restitution.

## (e) Verdict

**SHIP-WITH-CAVEATS.** The story rests on four NY Attorney General releases, an AG case page, and the appellate court's own decision. That is multiple primary sources, the villain is a company held liable by a court, and the victims are small businesses. The vivid details hold up at primary grade: the separate court calendar [7], the credit-report discovery [1], the few-hundred-dollar machine vs. thousands in payments [1], the spinoffs "risen on [its] ashes" [5], and "$680 million … No funds have been collected" [8]. The caveats: (1) the scout's hook is false as written (see DO NOT SAY). (2) Keep the 30,000 lawsuits / 19,000 judgments (through 2015) / 29,617 vacated (2020) numbers distinct. (3) Say "ordered dissolved", not "dissolved". (4) Date-stamp the "no funds collected" line to January 2025. Do not characterize the blocked 2025 and 2026 appellate rulings.
