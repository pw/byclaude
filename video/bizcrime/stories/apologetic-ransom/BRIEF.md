## (a) Verified summary

In the week after the Fourth of July weekend in 2022, the Houston restaurant Bludorn, co-owned by chef Aaron Bludorn and Cherif Mbodji, began getting one-star Google ratings with no comments attached, "all at the same time" [1][2]. Then came an email [2]: "Hello. Unfortunately negative feedback about your establishment has been left by us. And will appear in the future, one review a day... We sincerely apologize for our actions, I would not want to harm your business, but we have no other choice." It asked for a $75 Google Play gift card, linked to PayPal to buy it, and said the sale of the card would give the sender's family "three weeks of income" [2]. (The Houston Chronicle's printing of the same note adds: "We are begging you to send us Google Play gift card worth $75" and "After selling this gift card we can earn approximately $50" [5].) The note was a form letter: the New York Times reported "The text threat was the same in each email," sent from several Gmail accounts to restaurants from San Francisco to New York [7]. Bludorn's rating fell from 4.8 to 4.5 in one week [2]. Google initially said the ratings "didn't violate policy because there were no comments included" [1]. The owners refused to pay — "I didn't consider ever paying that ransom because to me that meant they would have, you know, won" [1] — posted the note publicly, and customers answered with about a hundred positive reviews [1][2]. When Google finally cleared the fake ratings, it removed the real good ones too [1][2]; the rating settled at 4.6, two-tenths below where it started [2].

## (b) Hook candidates

1. **"'We sincerely apologize for our actions.' The next sentence asked for seventy-five dollars."** — [2]: the note to Bludorn, "We sincerely apologize for our actions, I would not want to harm your business, but we have no other choice," and "The scammer went on to ask for $75 to be paid as a Google Play gift card." *Recommended.* Literally true of the note Bludorn received.
2. **"The email did its own math: a $75 gift card, resold, was 'three weeks of income.'"** — [2]: "with the proceeds from the sale of that gift card their family will have 'three weeks of income.'" (The "$50" figure is in [5]'s printing of the template, not in [2]'s account of Bludorn's copy; if used, say "the note said" and source it to [5].)
3. **"'Unfortunately negative feedback about your establishment has been left by us. And will appear in the future, one review a day.'"** — [2], verbatim opening of the email to Bludorn. Strong cold open because it reads like a customer-service letter.

Closing mechanism (supported): one-star ratings with no words attached, which Google at first said broke no rule [1][4], followed by an apologetic form letter sent to many restaurants at once [7], asking for a gift card because "such transactions are difficult to trace" ([7], a consultant's view — attribute it).

## (e) Verdict

**SHIP-WITH-CAVEATS.** The vivid detail survives and is better than the scout had it: the apology is verbatim in the note Bludorn received ("We sincerely apologize for our actions… we have no other choice") [2], matched by the Chronicle's printing [5] and the NYT's statement that the same text went to every target [7] — which turns "strange apologetic criminal" into the sharper, true point that the remorse was a *template*. Four independent outlets name Bludorn [1][2][5][6]; the villain is a scheme, not a private misfortune. Caveats: (1) no court or agency record exists and nobody was ever identified — the film cannot have a resolution and must not imply one; (2) the scout's primary source (CBS) doesn't carry the hook — claims.json must cite TODAY [2] for the note; (3) "sorry" belongs to other restaurants' notes, not Bludorn's; (4) the review count is disputed (a few vs. 30+) — give none; (5) "India" and "three weeks of income" are the scammer's claims, only ever "the note said."
