## (a) Verified summary

On Saturday, April 22, 2023, at about 10:37 a.m. [8], during the busiest stretch of the morning [9][10],
a man in a black baseball cap carrying a notepad [4][6] walked into Pearl's Bagels in Northwest D.C.
He told the manager he was there to inspect the ANSUL fire-suppression system [3][4]. He said he was from
the outside company that actually tags the shop's equipment [4][9][10]. He used the owner's name and
said the owner must have forgotten to mention he was coming [3]. He asked to speak to the owner, and the
manager called him [4]. On that call he gave the name "Jim Stance" and told owner Oliver Cox he would
update the tags and come back Monday with new parts [4]. The ANSUL system really was due for service [4],
and Cox had recently called about it [6], so nothing sounded wrong. The manager didn't hear the call [4].
After he hung up, the man told her Cox had said to pay the invoices in cash [8][9]. She paid him $970
from the register [3][9] against four invoices from "Metro Fire Prevention" [6][10]. The shop says that
company doesn't exist [10], and the invoice address, "1231 State St." in D.C. [4], had no quadrant [3].
By Cox's account he walked in around 10:35 and was gone at 10:43 [4]. The con came apart by text: the
manager wrote "I paid him cash like you said," Cox replied that he hadn't said that, and she answered
"oh no, he said, you said that" [3]. Cox called it "a little bit of Jedi Mind Trick" [3].

MPD investigated it as first-degree fraud, with a reward of up to $1,000 [4][5][9]. On April 24, WUSA9
reported that no arrests had been made [9]. On May 3, WJLA reported that police had named a suspect, wanted
on a D.C. Superior Court warrant for the case [5]. FOX 5 counted at least six similar incidents up and down
the East Coast, often under the same fake company name, and said it had not been determined that one man
was behind them all [3]. A Manhattan bagel shop owner said the same thing happened to him [3], and his
invoice carried the same fake address [4].

## (c) DO NOT SAY

2. **"...lied to the EMPLOYEE about what the owner had said, live, over the phone."** Wrong on timing. The
   lie to the manager came after the call ended: "when he got off the phone he lied to them" [8]; "then hung
   up and told our her we had said to just pay the invoices in cash" [9]. The manager "did not hear the phone
   conversation" [4]. Correct shape: he got the owner on the phone, hung up, then told the manager the owner
   had OK'd cash.
3. **"...got Cox on the phone and lied that Cox had authorized payment" (summary).** Don't imply he lied to

5. **"Cox later learned at least six similar... cons had hit businesses along the East Coast."** The

## (e) Verdict

**SHIP-WITH-CAVEATS.** The story rests on multiple independent sources, including police text (the MPD
release [8] and incident report [9]) and the owner's own on-record accounts. The villain is a con man
working a repeatable script, not a private misfortune. The vivid detail that makes it a video (the
"I paid him cash like you said" / "he said, you said that" text exchange) is on the record in the owner's
own words [3]. Caveats that must hold in the script:
- Rebuild the hook. The con is not "he never lied to the owner". It is: **he lied to each person
  separately.** To the owner he was a routine service tech coming back Monday. To the manager, after he hung
  up, he was a man the owner had just OK'd for cash. Neither heard the other's version. That's the true
  mechanism, and it's a better lesson for owners anyway: the phone call is what made the lie believable.
- $970, April 22 2023 (or "a Saturday in April 2023"), "Northwest D.C.". Say "fraud", not "robbery".
- Attribute the pattern ("at least six", same fake company) to local reporters, and keep the "not
  determined it's the same man" hedge.
- No arrest, charge outcome, or conviction for Pearl's is on record. Keep the suspect unnamed (recommended)
  or name him only as "wanted on a warrant".
