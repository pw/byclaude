- "On 30 June 1908, something exploded over Siberia": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunguska_event (30 June [O.S. 17 June] 1908, ~7:14 KRAT, Central Siberia); https://www.history.com/articles/tunguska-event-cause-asteroid (June 30, 1908)
- "flattened about 2,150 square kilometres of forest": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunguska_event ("nearly 2,150 km2 (830 sq mi) of forest in a radial pattern"); History.com ("more than 800 square miles"). Deliberately NOT using the "80 million trees" figure: Wikipedia's "Erroneous statements" section traces it to a 1982 non-peer-reviewed creationist paper (Boslough & Bruno 2025).
- "Kulik's expedition reached the centre in 1927; no crater; trees stripped and left standing; fallen trees radial": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunguska_event ("expected to find an impact crater... no crater at ground zero... zone roughly 8 km across where trees were scorched and devoid of branches, but still standing upright. Trees farther from the centre... knocked down away from the centre"); History.com (Kulik 1927, no crater, no meteorite fragments).
- "accepted explanation = asteroid or comet fragment bursting in the air; no piece formally identified": Wikipedia ("scientific consensus... meteor air burst... although no fragment has been formally identified"; 50–60 m body, burst at 5–10 km, ~10–15 Mt); History.com ("most scientists now think an asteroid exploded several miles above the ground"). Comet-fragment alternative: Drobyshevski, via Universe Today https://www.universetoday.com/146111/the-tunguska-explosion-could-have-been-caused-by-an-asteroid-that-still-orbits-the-sun/
- 2024–2026 check: no "solved"/overturning development found; History.com overview updated 2026-10-16 still gives airburst as consensus. Fringe/disputed items (Lake Cheko fragment, 2020 grazing-iron-meteor paper) are not in the caption.
