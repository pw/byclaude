# Marinara (episode 1) — sources

Fact-check log: three blind, tool-equipped rounds (`claude -p`, Opus 5.5, web search + fetch, no access to these sources): round 1 NEEDS FIXES (8 wording/adjacency items), round 2 NEEDS FIXES (4), round 3 **CLEAN**. Reports in `factcheck/round{1,2,3}.md`.

Every history claim in the narration and on screen, with the source and the words it rests on.

1. **Tomatoes reached Europe from the Americas in the 1500s; first European description 1544.**
   - van Andel et al., "Sixteenth-century tomatoes in Europe: who saw them, what they looked like, and where they came from," *PeerJ* 2022, doi:10.7717/peerj.12790 — "Soon after the Spanish conquest of the Americas, the first tomatoes were presented as curiosities to the European elite… Pietro Andrea Matthioli gave the first description of a tomato in 1544."
   - Wikipedia, "Tomato" — "The earliest mention of the tomato in European literature appeared in Pietro Andrea Mattioli's 1544 herbal."; "the tomato was brought to Europe in the Columbian exchange."
2a. **Some Italians ate them early (fried).**
   - Mattioli's 1544 herbal describes the fruit eaten fried in oil with salt and pepper, like eggplant — via "The first mention of a tomato (1544)," Research Square, https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-715398/v1 (McCue's translation); Smithsonian, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/why-the-tomato-was-feared-in-europe-for-more-than-200-years-863735/ (Gerard, 1597: "Spaniards and Italians" ate them).
2. **Many grew it for looks (ornamental/curiosity) rather than food; it is related to deadly nightshade and was suspected.**
   - Wikipedia, "Tomato" — "Elsewhere in Europe, its first use was ornamental, not least because it was understood to be related to the nightshades … assumed to be poisonous."
   - van Andel et al. 2022 (above) — "presented as curiosities to the European elite."
   - University of Vermont Extension, "History of tomatoes" (Deborah J. Benoit), https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/history-tomatoes — tomatoes are in the nightshade family with belladonna (deadly nightshade) and mandrake; cultivated in Europe "primarily as an ornamental plant."
   - Botany: tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) and deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*) are both in the family Solanaceae.
3. **1692, Naples: Antonio Latini, *Lo scalco alla moderna*, a tomato sauce "alla spagnuola" (Spanish-style), among the earliest printed tomato sauce recipes; served with boiled meats, not pasta.**
   - Wikipedia, "Antonio Latini" — tomato recipes in "the first volume" (1692) of *Lo scalco alla moderna* (Naples; vol. II 1694); one is "alla spagnuola" ("in the Spanish style"); employer Don Stefano Carillo Salcedo, first minister to the Spanish viceroy of Naples; Latini "did not suggest serving it over pasta."
   - Wikipedia, "Tomato" — "The earliest discovered cookbook with tomato recipes was published in Naples in 1692."
   - Wikipedia, "Tomato sauce" — "The first Italian recipe of tomato sauce was published under the name 'Spanish tomato sauce'" in *Lo Scalco alla Moderna* (1692 and 1694).
   - Accademia Italiana della Cucina (https://www.accademiaitalianadellacucina.it/sites/default/files/articoli_files/146_14.pdf) and Crystal King's translation (https://crystallyn.substack.com/p/the-worlds-first-tomato-sauce-recipe) — a sauce/condiment for boiled meats. (King cites Vol. II p.380; most sources place it in vol. I, 1692. The film says "1692" only.)
4. **"Marinara" = "sailor-style"; the sailor origin stories are tradition, not documented.**
   - Wikipedia, "Pizza marinara" — name "from Italian marinaro 'pertaining to sailors'"; the sailor-origin account "rests predominantly on oral and traditional retellings rather than documented, empirical evidence."
   - Wikipedia, "Marinara sauce" — "alla marinara ('sailor's style')"; the ship-cook and sailors'-wives origins are given as "folk theories."
5. **In Naples, marinara is a pizza: tomato, garlic, oregano, olive oil, no cheese.**
   - Commission Regulation (EU) No 97/2010, Pizza Napoletana TSG, https://legislation.gov.uk/eur/2010/97/annexes/data.xht — the Marinara topping: tomato, salt, oregano, sliced garlic, extra virgin olive oil (no cheese; mozzarella appears only in the Margherita variants).
   - Wikipedia, "Pizza marinara" — "seasoned with tomatoes, extra virgin olive oil, oregano, and garlic."
6. **In America, "marinara" names the tomato sauce.**
   - Wikipedia, "Marinara sauce" — "Widely used in Italian-American cuisine"; in central Italy a similar sauce is "sugo finto."
