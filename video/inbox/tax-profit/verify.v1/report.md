Two lines need a fix. The arithmetic, the form name and the example expenses all check out against IRS.gov. The problem is that the film tells viewers any expense they have a receipt for is deductible. The IRS test is whether the expense is "ordinary and necessary," and the personal part of an expense never comes off. I checked against the 2025 Schedule C instructions, Pub 334 (2025) and Pub 970 (2025).

### Checked and correct
- **Arithmetic:** 48,000 − 12,000 − 3,000 − 600 = 32,400. ✔
- **"net profit · Schedule C, line 31":** the 2025 Schedule C instructions put net profit or loss on line 31. ✔
- **Booth rent:** deductible. Pub 334: "you can deduct rent as a business expense only if the rent is for property you use in your business" (Schedule C line 20b). ✔
- **Color and supplies:** deductible. Schedule C instructions, line 22: "only to the extent you actually consumed and used them in your business during the tax year." ✔
- **Skills class:** deductible for a self-employed person as work-related education (Pub 970, details under the third finding). ✔
- **"Your federal tax starts from profit":** fair for both income tax and SE tax. Pub 970: "you deduct your expenses for qualifying work-related education directly from your self-employment income. This reduces the amount of your income subject to both income tax and self-employment tax." Since it says "starts from," it doesn't claim 32,400 is the taxable amount. ✔
- **Order and labelling:** the "example" label comes before the numbers, and the hook's question is answered by the math that follows. That order doesn't mislead. ✔

### Findings

**1. "Every business expense you can back up comes off the top."** Needs a fix.
- **What's wrong:** The sentence makes having a receipt the test. The real test is ordinary and necessary, and there are exceptions this audience actually runs into:
  - A mixed-use phone or car only counts for its business share.
  - Meals are limited to 50%.
  - Equipment with a useful life beyond the year is depreciated rather than taken all at once. The de minimis safe harbor often covers small items, but not always.
- A stylist could fairly read this line as "if I kept the receipt, it's deductible."
- **Source:** https://www.irs.gov/publications/p334
  - "To be deductible, a business expense must be both ordinary and necessary. An ordinary expense is one that is common and accepted in your field of business. A necessary expense is one that is helpful and appropriate for your business."
  - "If you have an expense that is partly for business and partly personal, separate the personal part from the business part."
  - "Deductions for meal expenses generally remain limited to 50% of such expenses."
  - Depreciable property "must have a useful life that extends substantially beyond the year it is placed in service."
- **Replacement:** "Every ordinary, necessary business expense you can back up comes out of profit."

**2. "It comes off before the tax is figured."** Needs a fix.
- **What's wrong:** "It" refers back to "every business expense," so this repeats finding 1's universal as the closing takeaway, which is the line viewers remember. It also has the same rule-of-thumb problem with equipment: that cost is spread over several years rather than coming off in one.
- **Source:** same as finding 1.
- **Replacement:** "Deductible business expenses come off before the tax is figured."

**3. "skills class 600"** Optional nit, not a fix.
- **What's wrong:** The line is true, and the row is marked as an example, so it's fine as is. But the deduction has a condition this audience could miss: the class has to maintain or improve skills in the work you already do. A course that qualifies you for a new trade (say, a stylist training as an esthetician) doesn't count.
- **Source:** https://www.irs.gov/publications/p970
  - Qualifying education "maintains or improves skills needed in your present work."
  - It "isn't qualifying work-related education if it: Is needed to meet the minimum educational requirements of your present trade or business, or Is part of a program of study that will qualify you for a new trade or business."
- **Replacement (optional):** "skills class (current trade)".

**4. "Forget one, and it stays in profit — taxed as if you kept it."** Nit, not a fix.
- **What's wrong:** True for the return as filed. A missed expense can be claimed later on an amended return, so "forget" isn't permanent. That doesn't mislead anyone into a wrong action, so no change is needed.

**5. Caption: "money in, minus business expenses."** Nit, not a fix.
- **What's wrong:** Strictly, Schedule C starts from gross receipts minus returns and cost of goods sold, which matters to someone who sells retail product. This is a fine simplification for the caption.

### What the film doesn't tell anyone to do
"Track every business expense" is a general habit that matches the IRS recordkeeping expectation, not advice to a particular person. The disclaimer lines are present on screen and in the caption.
