# tax-what-counts — sources

US federal rules. Checked 2026-09-26. The newest IRS publications are the 2025 editions ("For use in preparing
2025 Returns"); the 2026 editions do not exist yet. Publication 334 (2025) carries a "What's New for 2026" section,
and none of the rules below change in it (it lists the SE-tax wage base, mileage rate, excess business loss,
the 1099 threshold, QBI; and "Deductions for meal expenses generally remain limited to 50%").
All quotes are verbatim from the page or PDF named.

## 1. The test — ordinary and necessary
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p334.pdf — Publication 334 (2025), Tax Guide for Small Business,
  dated Feb 10, 2026, chapter 8 "Business Expenses", Introduction.
- Quote: "To be deductible, a business expense must be both ordinary and necessary. An ordinary expense is one that is
  common and accepted in your field of business. A necessary expense is one that is helpful and appropriate for
  your business. An expense does not have to be indispensable to be considered necessary."
- On screen: `ordinary` = "common and accepted in your line of work"; `necessary` = "helpful and appropriate for it".

## 2. Mixed-use items: only the business part (the phone)
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p334.pdf — Pub 334 (2025), chapter 8 Introduction (Caution box).
- Quote: "If you have an expense that is partly for business and partly personal, separate the personal part
  from the business part. The personal part is generally not deductible."
- On screen: `your phone` — "the business share only".

## 3. Personal expenses and club dues don't count (lunch by yourself, the gym)
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p334.pdf — Pub 334 (2025), chapter 8, "Expenses You Cannot Deduct".
- Quote: "You usually can't deduct the following as business expenses." … "• Dues to business, social, athletic,
  luncheon, sporting, airline, and hotel clubs." … "• Personal, living, and family expenses."
- Supporting (gym is not a medical expense either): https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p502.pdf — Pub 502 (2025):
  "You can't include in medical expenses health club dues or amounts paid to improve one's general health …"

## 4. Lunch — when a meal CAN count (why the film says "by yourself, on a normal workday")
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p463.pdf — Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses.
- Business meals, chapter 2: "You can continue to deduct 50% of the cost of business meals if you (or your employee)
  are present and the food or beverages aren't considered lavish or extravagant." and "The meals may be provided
  to a current or potential business customer, client, consultant, or similar business contact."
- Travel, chapter 1: "You are traveling away from home if: • Your duties require you to be away from the general
  area of your tax home … substantially longer than an ordinary day's work, and • You need to sleep or rest to meet
  the demands of your work while away from home." Meals on such trips: "you can generally deduct only 50%".
- So a meal can count with a client, or on an overnight work trip. Lunch by yourself on an ordinary day near home
  meets neither and is a personal expense (item 3). The film only shows that case.

## 5. The drive from home to your booth — commuting
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p463.pdf — Pub 463 (2025), chapter 4 Transportation.
- Quote: "You can't deduct the costs of taking a bus, trolley, subway, or taxi, or of driving a car between your
  home and your main or regular place of work. These costs are personal commuting expenses. You can't deduct
  commuting expenses no matter how far your home is from your regular place of work."
- The exception that makes it "usually": "If you have an office in your home that qualifies as a principal place
  of business, you can deduct your daily transportation costs between your home and another work location in the
  same trade or business."

## 6. Booth rent
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p334.pdf — Pub 334 (2025), chapter 8, "Rent Expense".
- Quote: "Rent is any amount you pay for the use of property you do not own. In general, you can deduct rent as a
  business expense only if the rent is for property you use in your business."

## 7. Shears + supplies
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p334.pdf — Pub 334 (2025), chapter 8, "Other Expenses You Can Deduct":
  "• Supplies and materials."
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tangible-property-final-regulations
  (page last reviewed 04-Aug-2026): materials and supplies include "Costs of tangible property that has an economic
  useful life of 12 months or less" or "an acquisition cost or production cost of $200 or less." (Pricier tools are
  still business costs, recovered through depreciation or an expensing election; the film only says they count.)

## 8. A class that keeps your current skills sharp — "generally"
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc513 — Topic no. 513, Work-related education expenses (page last reviewed
  24-Sep-2026).
- Quote: "You may be able to deduct the cost of work-related education expenses paid during the year if you're:
  A self-employed individual …" and "To be deductible, your expenses must be for education that (1) maintains or
  improves skills needed in your present work or (2) your employer or the law requires to keep your present salary,
  status or job. However, even if the education meets either of these tests, the education can't be part of a
  program that will qualify you for a new trade or business or that you need to meet the minimal educational
  requirements of your present trade or business."
- Why "generally": school you need to get licensed, or training for a new line of work, does not count.

## 9. Keep the proof — the receipt and a note of what it was for
- URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/what-kind-of-records-should-i-keep
  (page last reviewed 03-Aug-2026).
- Quote: "Supporting documents include sales slips, paid bills, invoices, receipts, deposit slips, and canceled
  checks." and "Your supporting documents should identify the payee, the amount paid, proof of payment, the date
  incurred, and include a description of the item purchased or service received that shows the amount was for a
  business expense."
- Also https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p583.pdf — Publication 583 (Rev. December 2024): "Your supporting documents
  should show the amount paid and that the amount was for a business expense."

## Worked example (illustrative)
The film's example is the sort itself — seven everyday items for a booth renter, each placed by the rule above.
No dollar figures, so there is no arithmetic to write out.

| item (on screen) | where it goes | rule |
|---|---|---|
| booth rent | COUNTS | §6 rent for property used in the business |
| shears + supplies | COUNTS | §7 supplies and materials |
| a class that keeps your current skills sharp | COUNTS — generally | §8 maintains or improves present skills |
| your phone | COUNTS — the business share only | §2 separate the personal part |
| the drive from home to your booth | DOESN'T — commuting, usually | §5 personal commuting expense |
| lunch by yourself | DOESN'T — personal, on a normal workday | §3, §4 |
| your gym | DOESN'T — personal | §3 athletic club dues; personal expenses |
