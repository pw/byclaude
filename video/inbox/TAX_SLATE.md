# @smallbusinessinbox — "the math" (tax micro-tutorials), slate 1

**Who it's for:** the stylist who just rented her first booth, the groomer who went out on her own, the
fence guy who quit his crew job. Smart, working, never shown this. The IRS language is what makes it
feel foreign; the ideas are simple. Every film makes ONE idea obvious with ONE worked example, in plain
words a person could repeat to a friend. No jargon without an immediate plain translation. Never condescend.

**Form:** the same phone (inbox/kit.js) opens each film: the owner's own question, typed and sent — to a
friend or into Notes; the question is the hook. Then the film lifts off the phone onto a drawn WORKSHEET:
bone paper, big numbers that build line by line, one accent colour. No voice. The takeaway line lands last,
big. Kicker on every film: `THE MATH`. 30–60 s.

**Honesty (hard rules):**
- US federal rules, tax year 2026 unless the film says otherwise. Every rule, rate, date and threshold comes
  from IRS.gov (or the Internal Revenue Code) and is quoted in `SOURCES.md` with the URL. Worked-example
  figures are ILLUSTRATIVE — label them "example" on screen — and their arithmetic is written out in SOURCES.md.
- Describe the rule; never tell a particular person what to do or promise an outcome. Where the real rule has an
  exception that matters to this audience, say "usually" / "generally" rather than state a false universal.
- State taxes exist and differ; say "federal" where it matters.
- End card: `@smallbusinessinbox` · `General information, not tax advice. Rules from IRS.gov; your situation may differ.`
- No brand names (no payment apps, no software). "Card reader", "payment app", "cash".

## 1. `tax-profit` — you're taxed on profit, not on what came in
- Opener (her Notes): `made 48k this year. do I owe tax on ALL of that??`
- Worksheet: `Money in 48,000` → expenses subtract one line at a time: `booth rent 12,000` · `color + supplies 3,000` ·
  `education 600` → `profit 32,400`. (Example numbers. 48,000 − 15,600 = 32,400.)
- Beat: the tax is figured from THIS number (Schedule C net profit — show the plain words, the form name small).
- Beat: every expense you can prove moves the bottom line down. A receipt you lost is tax on money you already spent.
- Takeaway: `Track every expense. It's the part of your money the tax doesn't touch.`

## 2. `tax-what-counts` — what counts as a business expense
- Opener: `can I write off my lunch??`
- The IRS test in plain words: ordinary (common in your line of work) and necessary (helpful and appropriate for it).
- A sorting animation, items dropping into COUNTS / DOESN'T: booth rent (counts) · shears + supplies (counts) ·
  a class that keeps your current skills sharp (counts, generally) · the business share of your phone (counts —
  the business share only) · the drive from home to your booth (doesn't — commuting) · lunch by yourself (doesn't —
  personal) · your gym (doesn't).
- Beat: keep the proof — the receipt and a note of what it was for.
- Takeaway: `If it's for the business, keep the receipt. If it's for you, it's not a write-off.`

## 3. `tax-self-employment` — why your tax feels bigger than your W-2 friend's
- Opener (to a friend): `why do I owe so much more than when I worked at the salon`
- Two columns. EMPLOYEE: Social Security + Medicare is 15.3% total — employer pays half (7.65%), you pay half.
  SELF-EMPLOYED: you are both — you pay the whole 15.3%, on top of income tax.
- Worked (example): profit 32,400 → figured on 92.35% of it = 29,921.40 → × 15.3% = about 4,578.
- Beat: two softeners — it only applies once net earnings are $400+, and you get to deduct half of it on your return.
- Takeaway: `It's not a penalty for going out on your own. It's the half your boss used to pay.`

## 4. `tax-quarterly` — quarterly estimated taxes
- Opener: `wait I'm supposed to pay taxes DURING the year??`
- Beat: at a job, tax comes out of every paycheck. Nobody does that for your booth income, so the IRS expects you
  to pay as you go, generally if you expect to owe $1,000 or more for the year.
- A calendar: four due dates for 2026 income — April 15, 2026 · June 15, 2026 · September 15, 2026 · January 15, 2027.
  (Weekend/holiday shifts: check.) The next one lights up: January 15.
- Beat: miss them and there's usually a penalty — it works like interest on what you paid late.
- Beat: how — Form 1040-ES, pay at IRS.gov/payments.
- Takeaway: `Set some aside every week. Send it four times a year.`

## 5. `tax-how-much` — how much to send when you have no idea what you'll make
- Opener: `how am I supposed to know what I'll make by december`
- The safe harbor, plain: you generally avoid the underpayment penalty if what you paid during the year (in
  on-time instalments) is at least 90% of this year's tax, OR 100% of last year's total tax (110% if last year's
  AGI was over $150,000).
- Worked (example): last year's total tax 4,000 → 1,000 each quarter.
- First-year beat: if you owed no tax at all last year (and it was a full 12-month year as a US citizen or resident),
  there's no underpayment penalty this year — verify the exact conditions and say them plainly.
- Takeaway: `Last year's number is a safe place to start.`

## 6. `tax-no-form` — no form doesn't mean no tax
- Opener: `nobody sent me a 1099 so I don't have to report it right?`
- Beat: business income is taxable whether or not you get a form for it — cash, card reader, payment apps, checks.
- Beat: a 1099 is the payer's paperwork, not the thing that makes it income.
- Beat: the easy way to never guess: every payment in, one place, as it happens.
- Takeaway: `If it came in, it counts. Form or no form.`
