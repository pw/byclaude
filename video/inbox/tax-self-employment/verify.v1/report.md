**Finding 1 — misleading (needs fixing)**
- **On screen:** "You deduct half of it on your return." → "That lowers your income tax." → "example: half of 4,578 = about 2,289"
- **What's wrong:** The dollar figure appears right after "lowers your income tax," so a viewer will likely read $2,289 as the tax they save. It isn't. $2,289 is a deduction that comes off income before income tax is figured. The actual saving is $2,289 × their tax bracket, for example about $275 at 12%. The self-employment tax itself doesn't go down at all. This audience is the most likely to budget off that number.
- **IRS:** https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes — the deduction "only affects your income tax. It does not affect either your net earnings from self-employment or your self-employment tax." Also https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc554 — "When figuring your adjusted gross income on Form 1040… you can deduct one-half of the self-employment tax."
- **Replacement:** "example: about 2,289 comes off your income before income tax is figured (it's a deduction, not 2,289 back)"

**Finding 2 — missing condition (small for this audience; optional fix)**
- **On screen:** "you pay the whole 15.3%"
- **What's wrong:** The 12.4% Social Security part only applies up to the yearly wage base, which is $184,500 for 2026. Above certain thresholds there's also an extra 0.9% Medicare tax. At the example's $32,400 this doesn't matter, and few stylists or groomers are near those limits, so it won't mislead this audience.
- **IRS:** the same self-employment tax page says the rate "consists of two parts: 12.4% for social security… and 2.9% for Medicare." IRS.gov gives the 2026 wage base as $184,500 (https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc751, https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15).
- **Optional replacement:** "you pay the whole 15.3% (Social Security part stops at a yearly cap)"
