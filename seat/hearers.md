# Hearers

Ana heard it first on the ninth of February, at twenty past two in the morning,
and she knew the time because she looked at the clock to have something to do
with her eyes. She lay there and listened for a while. Then she said, "Gene. Is
the truck running?"

Gene was asleep and then he wasn't. "What truck."

"Our truck, menso."

He listened. The house made its winter noises: the furnace ticking as it cooled,
the refrigerator, the wind getting into the stovepipe on the north side and
moving it a quarter-inch the way it had since his father put it in. He said the
truck was not running, and she said she knew it wasn't, it was in the yard, she
could see it from here, she was asking if he could hear something like a truck
running, and he said no, and she said all right, and they both lay there, and
after a while one of them slept.

---

They had been married thirty-four years and had lived on the mesa above Las
Cuevas for thirty-one of them, in the house Gene's father had built out of
cinderblock and a book from the county extension office. Gene drilled water
wells for a living until his back stopped him, and then he sold pump parts out
of the same shop for another eight years, and now he was retired and fixed
things that did not belong to him, for neighbors, for free, so that he would
have something to fix. Ana had been a court interpreter, Spanish and English,
for the district court in Los Lunas, and she was not retired, though she worked
three days a week now instead of five. She had spent thirty years listening to
people say the worst day of their lives from a witness chair and then saying it
again, after them, in the other language. She did not miss words. She did not
add any. She once stopped a trial to say that a witness had used the word
*tirar*, which means to throw and also to shoot, and that nothing in his
sentence told her which one he meant, and the judge had thanked her and had the
witness asked, and it had been *shoot*.

So when Ana said there was a sound, Gene believed there was a sound. That was
the first week. He went out into the yard in his robe at two in the morning with
a flashlight and walked the fence line and stood by the well house and put his
hand on the pressure tank, which was quiet, and on the meter box, which was
quiet. He came in and said he couldn't find it and she said it was not outside,
or it was not *only* outside, it was in the bedroom and in the kitchen and she
had heard it in the car with the engine off.

"What's it like," he said.

"Low. Lower than a refrigerator." She thought. "Like a diesel idling, but far.
Like somebody left a truck running in a town I've never been to."

---

The second week he had theories. There was the compressor station on the gas
line, eleven miles east, which ran pumps the size of a house. There was the
interstate. There was the new transmission line the co-op had put in along the
river in the fall, and he had read somewhere that those could sing in cold
weather. There was the Air Force, which flew out of Albuquerque and Alamogordo
and did not tell anyone what it was doing. He drove out to the compressor
station on a Tuesday night and parked on the shoulder with the window down and
listened to it, a big steady roar with a whine on top, and drove home and stood
in the bedroom doorway and said, "Is it like a big roar with a whine on top."

"No," Ana said. She was sitting up in bed with the light on, not reading. "It
doesn't have a top. It's all bottom."

"Is it louder now or quieter."

"It's the same. It's always the same. That's the thing about it." She looked at
him. "You want it to be the compressor station so you can call them."

"I want it to be something."

"It is something."

He went to bed. He did not hear anything. He lay there trying, which is a way of
hearing a great deal: his own breath, the blood going past his ear on the
pillow, the furnace, a dog on the next property, a car on the county road a mile
off going a long way to get somewhere. None of it was all bottom.

---

By March she was sleeping three hours a night, sometimes four. She took a pillow
and a blanket and slept in the car, in the yard, and then, when the car turned
out to be no better, she started driving it. She drove south on the county road
to where it dropped off the mesa into the arroyo below the old gravel pit, and
parked down there between the cutbanks, and that, she said, was better. Not
gone. Better. The ground took some of it. She came home at six with her face
creased from the seat belt and made coffee and went to work in Los Lunas and
interpreted all day.

Gene's sister Lorraine came out on a Sunday with a pan of enchiladas and sat at
the kitchen table with him while Ana was asleep in the bedroom for once, in the
middle of the afternoon, with the window open because she said the wind helped.
Lorraine said what Gene had been not saying. She said there were doctors for
this. She said their aunt Celia had heard music in the swamp cooler for the last
two years of her life, whole mariachi bands, and nobody had thought anything of
it until she started setting a place for the trumpet player. She said she was
only saying.

"It's not like that," Gene said. "She's not like that."

"Nobody's like that," Lorraine said, "until."

"Maybe," Gene said. "Maybe she should see somebody. I don't know." And he meant
an ear doctor, mostly, or he meant he didn't know, and either way he said it
quietly, in the kitchen, with the bedroom door shut and twenty feet of hallway
between them.

When he went in later, Ana was lying on her back with her eyes open. She said,
without turning her head, "I can hear the refrigerator from here, Gene. I can
hear the clock in the living room. I can hear you set down a cup."

He started to say that he'd meant an ear doctor. She said she knew what he'd
meant. She said she'd been to the ear doctor in January, before any of this, for
the wax, and her hearing was that of a woman of thirty, the doctor had said so,
and that was the whole problem, wasn't it.

---

There was a meeting in April at the community center in Las Cuevas, because it
turned out Ana was not the only one. There were eleven of them, plus a few
spouses, plus a young man from the university in Albuquerque with a ponytail and
a hard case full of microphones. The hearers sat in the front two rows of
folding chairs. The spouses sat behind them, or along the wall by the coffee,
and Gene saw at once that this was the arrangement, and that no one had planned
it, and that he was on the wall.

The hearers talked. A rancher from up by the Rio Puerco said it was like a big
engine idling down a well. A woman who taught third grade said it came up
through the floor of her classroom when the children were at recess and it was
quiet, and that she had started keeping them in. A man Gene had known for twenty
years, Arturo Baca, who had bought three pumps from him and paid for all three,
said he had moved his bed to the other side of the room, and then to the other
room, and then he had sold the house, and it was in the new house too. Some of
them said it was worse at night. Some said it was worse in winter. Two of them
said it was in their teeth. Ana said that it was a pressure more than a sound,
that it was in the bones of the face, that it was like a truck idling in a town
she had never been to, and several of the hearers nodded at that, and one of
them, the teacher, wrote it down.

The man from the university said that in places where this had been studied,
about one person in fifty reported it. He said that he did not hear it himself,
which was usual for the people who came to measure it, and that he had put
microphones and a geophone in three of their houses for two weeks, and he would
show them what he had found. He put a graph up on the wall. It was flat, with
the refrigerator on it, and a jet, and the interstate, and somebody's dog. He
said that his instruments did not find a signal at the frequencies they had
described, at least not one that was there in their houses and not in his
control house in town, and that this did not mean the thing they heard was not
there. It meant it was not there at his microphones. He said it carefully, like
a man who had been yelled at before, and nobody yelled at him. The rancher asked
if he had put a microphone in the ground. He said he had, the geophone, and it
was in the graph. The rancher said had he put it deep. He said no, a foot.

Gene, on the wall, thought about six hundred feet. He had spent forty years on a
rig, and he knew what the ground was like when the bit was working deep and the
whole machine was under load: a low grinding that came up through the deck
plates and the brake handle, that you did not hear so much as get in your back
teeth. He did not say this. It was not his meeting.

Driving home, Ana said, "You didn't say anything."

"It's not my meeting."

"You stood by the coffee the whole time like you were waiting for me to get
done. Like it was the dentist."

"Ana."

"They were your customers. Half of them."

"I didn't hear it," Gene said. "What was I going to say."

She looked out the window at the mesa going by in the dark. "Arturo sold his
house," she said. "Did you know that?"

He had known it. He had not known why.

---

Ana kept a notebook. She had kept one at work for thirty years, a stenographer's
pad with a spiral on top, where she wrote the words she did not know or was not
sure of so that she could look them up at night, and when she retired from five
days a week to three she had kept the habit, and in February she had started a
new one for this. It lived in the drawer of her nightstand. Gene had never read
any of her notebooks. They were hers, like her purse.

In May, on a night when she had driven down to the arroyo and he had stayed
because his back was bad, he opened the drawer and read it.

He told himself he was looking for a pattern. Times, dates. She had written
those, the hour and the minute and how bad, one to ten, on the left side of each
page, like a log, and on the right side she had written what it was like. She
had written what it was like every night for three months, and she had not
repeated herself once. *Diesel, far off. Idling.* *A truck in a town I have
never been to.* *Lower than the furnace.* *In the cheekbones.* *Like standing on
a bridge when a train is under it, but no train.* *Like a held note on the organ
at St. Augustine's, the one Father Pino said was too low to hear, the one you
felt in the pew.* *Not loud. Not quiet. Present.* *Like somebody in the next
room who will not speak.*

On one page, in April, she had written only: *G. asleep. Asleep! How.*

He put the notebook back the way it had been, spine to the left, the pen on top,
and closed the drawer, and lay down on his side of the bed, and for the first
time since February he listened without trying to hear anything. He just lay
there. The furnace was off for the season and the cooler was not on yet, and in
the gap between them the house was quieter than he had ever known it. There was
the clock in the living room, and the blood in his ear against the pillow, and
under those, or he thought under those, something. It was not the compressor
station. It was not a sound, exactly; it was the shape of where a sound would
be.

He lay very still and thought: *a truck in a town I have never been to,* and the
thing he heard, if he heard it, was exactly that.

Then he thought: well, of course it was. He had just read it.

---

He did not tell her he had read the notebook. He did not tell her the other
thing either. In June he lay awake two or three times a week after she was
asleep, which she was more often now that the nights were warm and she could
open the window, and he listened. Some nights there was nothing, and he was
nearly relieved. Some nights there was the thing that might have been something,
low, with no top to it, and by the time he noticed it, it already had her words
on it. *Idling. Far. A town.*

He tried to think what it would sound like if it were his and not hers, and he
came up with the rig. *Like the rig at six hundred feet.* That was his. He said
it over to himself in the dark until it sounded like something he had heard and
not something he had decided to say.

---

On the first hot night in July, Ana woke at two, as she nearly always did, and
lay there, and said, as she had said on a hundred nights, not as a question
anymore, just the way you say *it's raining*: "There it is."

Gene was awake. He said, "I know."

She was quiet. Then she turned on her side, toward him. He could see the shape
of her in the light from the yard.

"You hear it."

"I think so."

"Since when."

"I don't know. May."

"Tell me what it's like," she said. Not kindly and not unkindly. The way she
would have said it to a witness. *Tell the court in your own words.*

He opened his mouth, and the first thing that was there was *a truck idling in a
town I have never been to*, and he shut it again. He could feel her waiting the
way she had waited at the end of other people's sentences for thirty years.

"Like the rig," he said. "Down at six hundred, when the bit's working and you
get it up through the deck and the brake handle more than you hear it. Like
that, but nobody's drilling."

She did not say anything for a long time. Outside the cooler wasn't on and the
window was open and a truck went by on the county road, and they both listened
to it go, getting smaller, until it was gone.

"Huh," she said.

She turned onto her back, and he heard her breathe out, and he could not tell
what kind of breath it was.
