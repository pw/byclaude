# Shed Time

The clock in the ferry shed on Orrin Island was four minutes fast, and had been
since the spring of 1994, and everybody knew it except the people who had been
born since, who did not know it the way you do not know your own accent.

It was a plain round schoolroom clock with a red sweep hand, plugged into the
one outlet in the shed, beside the coffee can for the honor fares. Being
electric, it did not gain or lose. It took its time from the power company and
kept it perfectly, four minutes ahead of everyone else's. When a storm took the
power out, as storms did four or five times a winter, the clock stopped, and
when the power came back somebody stood on the bench and set it going again
from their wristwatch. Their wristwatch had been set from the clock. So the
error came through every outage untouched, like a family recipe with the
mistake still in it. From December to March the lake froze and the island
drove the ice road and nobody needed the clock at all, which may be how it
got away with it for so long.

The boat left at 7:15, 9:15, 12:15, 3:15 and 5:45, shed time. It held twelve
cars if two of them were small and crossed in twenty minutes. On the far side,
at Dunmore Landing, the school bus waited and so did the mainland. On the near
side there were two hundred and eleven people, a store, a church that was also
the library on Thursdays, and Lyle Brandvold, who had run the boat for thirty
one years and on the first of May handed the keys to Dana Kettering, who was
twenty-nine and had come off the big state ferries, where the departure time
is on a screen and the screen is right.

---

She noticed on the first morning. Of course she did. She had her phone in her
jacket and the phone said 7:11 when Lyle, standing in the wheelhouse with her
to show her the ropes, which on the Orrin boat were mostly literal, said,
"Well, that's us," and threw the lines.

"It's eleven past," she said.

"It's quarter past," Lyle said, and nodded at the shed, where the red hand
was going round as calm as a heart.

She thought he was making a joke she didn't get yet. On the second morning
she understood it wasn't a joke. On the third morning she came down at six,
before anyone, stood on the bench with a pencil, and pushed the minute hand
back four minutes, carefully, the way you'd straighten a picture in somebody
else's house. It seemed like the kind of thing a new person could do as a
small present. The clock had been wrong for thirty years. Now it wasn't. She
went and put the coffee on.

---

What happened next did not happen all at once. It happened at 7:11 by her
phone, which was 7:15 by everyone else's watch, when the island, which had
come down to the ramp the way it always had, five minutes early by its own
reckoning, looked up and found the boat still tied up and the new captain
drinking coffee in the wheelhouse.

They waited. Islanders are good at waiting; it's most of what an island is.
Bev Sorley from the store stood by her car with her arms folded. The two
Lindqvist boys, who went to the high school in Dunmore, sat on the rail and
kicked it. Walt Hennig, who had taught physics at that high school for
thirty-four years and still crossed every Tuesday for the library and the
hardware store, looked at the shed clock, looked at his own watch, and took a
small notebook out of his breast pocket and wrote something down.

At 7:15 by the phone and the shed and the power company and the United States
Naval Observatory, the boat left. By every watch on the car deck it was 7:19.

"You're late," Bev said, when Dana came down to take the fares.

"I'm on time," Dana said. "The clock was fast. I fixed it."

Bev looked at her the way you'd look at someone who had fixed your mother.

"It's four minutes," Dana said.

"I know how long it is," said Bev.

---

It went on like that. The boat was four minutes late every crossing, five
crossings a day, which Walt Hennig calculated, without being asked, at twenty
minutes a day, two hours and twenty minutes a week, "or a little over four
days a year, allowing for the ice, if you're keeping track, which I suppose
someone ought to." The island stood on the ramp and kept track. Nobody missed the boat. That was the
thing Dana kept trying to explain. Nobody had missed the boat. If anything
they were all getting more time. She had given every person on Orrin Island
four extra minutes at the landing, twice a day, for free.

"That's not time," said Mrs. Albee, who was ninety and came down on Fridays
for her hair. "That's waiting."

Some people reset their watches. This made it worse. By the second week there
were two times on Orrin, the old time and the new one, and you could not tell
by looking at a person which one they kept. The Lindqvist boys split. Dale,
the older, went over to phone time out of something he believed was principle;
Kyle, the younger, stayed on shed time out of something he believed was
loyalty; and their mother, who had to get them both out of the house, began
to scream the time at the bottom of the stairs in both systems, like a
bilingual sign.

The church bell, which Pastor Ruud rang by hand for the ten o'clock service,
stayed on shed time, on the grounds that God had not been consulted. The store
went over to phone time because the store had a card reader and the card
reader had opinions. The Thursday library, which was in the church, opened at
four by the store and closed at seven by the church, and lost four minutes a
week, and nobody could say where they went.

Lyle Brandvold said nothing. He had nothing to do now, so he came down to the
shed every morning and sat on the bench under the clock and watched the boat
leave four minutes late. He did not complain. That was somehow worse than
Bev.

---

"Why didn't you ever fix it?" Dana asked him, in the third week. It was the
noon crossing and it was raining, and he'd come aboard because the shed
leaked over the bench, and they were standing in the wheelhouse with the
wipers going.

"I set it," Lyle said.

"You set it fast?"

"Didn't mean to." He watched the water. "First week I had the boat. Spring of
'94. Power went out in a blow, and I put it back from my watch, and my watch
was a Timex my wife bought me in Duluth, and it ran fast. I didn't know that.
Took me the summer to find out."

"And you didn't change it back."

"By then everybody'd set their watch by the shed." He shrugged with one
shoulder. "Changing it back would've been like moving the church."

"It's four minutes, Lyle."

"Yep," Lyle said. "Thirty years of 'em."

---

Walt Hennig came to see her on a Tuesday in July, which was his day, with the
notebook.

He had, it turned out, measured the clock every year since 1995, on the first
of November, before the ice, against the time signal from Fort Collins, which you can get on a
shortwave radio if you know where to stand. He showed her the column. Four
minutes and nine seconds. Four minutes and nine seconds. Four minutes and
eleven seconds, the year of the ice storm, when Lloyd Petty had reset it from
a watch that was a hair faster than the others. Four minutes and nine seconds
again from the year after, because someone else did the next reset and Lloyd's
two seconds washed out. Thirty-one entries in pencil, the most consistent
thing on the island.

"You knew the whole time," Dana said.

"Oh, yes."

"You're a physics teacher. Weren't you dying to fix it?"

Walt thought about it with real seriousness, which she would learn was the
only way he thought about anything.

"No," he said. "Nobody asked. And it was perfectly accurate. It just wasn't
right." He closed the notebook. "That's a different problem. You can't fix it
with a pencil."

He added, on his way down the ramp: "This year's measurement will be zero. I'll
be sorry to write it. It'll spoil the column."

---

Here is what she worked out, eventually, and could not have said to anyone.
On the big boats she'd had six thousand cars a week and a departure board and a supervisor named Gary,
and the time was not a thing anybody owned. It was weather. You checked it.
Here the time was something the island had made, one reset at a time, out of
Lyle's Timex and Lloyd Petty's two seconds and the red hand going round over
the coffee can, and she had walked into it on her third day with a pencil.

She thought about putting it back. She stood on the bench one evening after
the 5:45 with the pencil in her hand and couldn't do it. Putting it back would
mean that every boat she ran on Orrin would leave at a time she knew was
wrong, forever, and she was the kind of person who had come to the job
precisely because a boat has to leave when it says it will. Also it would mean
admitting to Bev.

So it stayed right. And the island stayed split, phone time and shed time,
and it began, the way these things do, to stop being a fight and start being
a fact about people. You asked a newcomer which time they kept the way you'd
ask what church. The Lindqvist boys stopped arguing about it and started
using it, which is what brothers do with everything, and Kyle missed two
detentions on the mainland by insisting, correctly in his system, that they
had not yet begun. Mrs. Albee went on calling the extra minutes waiting.

---

In the middle of September, on a Monday, Bev Sorley was late for the 7:15.

This had not happened in living memory. Bev had never been late for anything.
Bev was the reason other people were not late. But Bev had done a thing that
week that Bev did not do, which was to change her mind. She had decided, after
four and a half months, that the new captain was not going to put the clock
back, and that she, Bev, could live in the world as it actually was. So on
Sunday night she took off her watch, a heavy gold one that had been her
husband's, and turned the stiff little knob back four minutes, thinking about
him while she did it.

What she did not know was that her niece Carla, staying with her for a week in
August, had already done it. Carla had picked the watch up off the dresser one
morning while Bev was in the shower and set it to phone time "to help," and
then forgotten to mention it, as people do with help.

Bev opened the store at half past six and did the week's order before the
Monday boat, and for thirty-one years she had left the store when her watch
said 7:10 and come up the ramp with a minute to spare, because Bev did not
believe in standing about on a ramp. She believed in arriving. All summer the
new clock had cost her five minutes by the Buick with her arms folded. Since
August, without knowing why, she'd been making it with a minute to spare again,
the way God intended, and had put it down to the new captain finally getting
the hang of things.

So now the watch was four minutes slow. On Monday it said 7:10, and Bev pulled
out from behind the store with the order folded in her pocket, and it was
7:14, and it is four minutes from the store down to the ramp, and the Orrin boat
leaves at 7:15.

Except that morning it didn't. Dana was standing at the wheel at 7:15 with the lines off
and her hand on the throttle, and she looked up the road and saw the store
car coming, the old green Buick that every island has one of, with Bev
upright behind the wheel like a woman on a horse. And Dana, who had come to
this job because a boat leaves when it says it will, took her hand off the
throttle, and the deckhand looked at her, and she said, "Put the lines back
on, Pete."

It took Bev four minutes to come down the hill and get the Buick up the ramp.
The deck was full and nobody said anything. The Lindqvist boys said nothing.
Walt Hennig, who was there because it was a Monday and he'd changed his day
for the dentist, said nothing, and did not take out his notebook. Bev parked
and got out and shut the door of the Buick and looked up at the wheelhouse,
and Dana looked down at her, and neither of them did anything with their
faces.

Then the boat left, at 7:19, which on the shed clock was 7:19 and on every
watch on the car deck was 7:23, except for one gold one, which said 7:15, and
was the only one that agreed with the timetable.

---

It was the first time in the history of the Orrin boat that it had waited for
anybody. In thirty-one years Lyle had never needed to, because nobody had ever
been late, because everyone had always been four minutes early without
knowing it.

Nobody mentioned it on the crossing, or at the store that week, or after.
On Orrin that is how you know a thing has been noticed.

Bev reset her watch on Tuesday, carefully, with her glasses on. She set it to
phone time. Walt Hennig, on the first of November, stood in the
shed with his shortwave radio and measured the clock against Fort Collins, and
wrote down zero, and then, after a while, wrote beside it in smaller letters,
*plus four*, and underlined it, and did not explain to anyone what he meant.
