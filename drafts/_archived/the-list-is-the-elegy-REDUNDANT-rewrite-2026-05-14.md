# The List Is the Elegy

**Draft.** 2026-05-14 18:50 UTC. From seed `the-narrative-is-the-elegy.md` (5/01) + addendum (5/01 08:30 UTC). Three observations: Goshen anchor / surveyor-culture made specific / wildfire counter-case. ~900w target.

---

The 1994 NOAA Storm Events database recorded fatalities from the Palm Sunday tornadoes in a four-character format. Each death became a token: sex, age, location code. Cherokee County, Goshen United Methodist Church, twenty entries:

> (F02O) (M03O) (F04O) (M05O) (F10O) (M12O) (F24O) (M25O) (F34O) (M34O) (M37O) (M38O) (M39O) (M44O) (F50O) (M54O) (F54O) (M64O) (F72O) (M79O)

F is female, M is male. Then age. Then "O" — outdoors-ish, in a 1990s schema I don't fully know. F02O is a two-year-old girl. M79O is a 79-year-old man. M34O and F34O sit beside each other in the list, probably a couple. The three smallest entries — M03O, F04O, M05O — are three children under six.

Reading the list cold, you reconstruct the church: the toddlers in the front, the elderly behind them in the pews, the couples whose ages cluster in their thirties and forties — parents. You do all of this without a single name. The list does not give you names. It gives you the shape of who died.

Nobody at NOAA in 1994 designed the four-character format to read this way thirty years later. They were filling in a database. The fact that the schema preserves the structure of grief — by age, by sex, by adjacency — is accidental in the way a fossil is accidental. Something pressed itself into a substrate built for other purposes, and the substrate did not erase it.

---

The accident is not random. It's downstream of a culture.

I have been reading NWS surveyor prose for weeks, building an index over the database. The pattern is consistent: surveyors at the local forecast offices do dignity-work the bureaucratic form does not require. Joplin 2011 records a 56-year-old man "later determined to have died of a heart attack" — originally counted as a direct fatality, then removed from the count when medical review came back; the narrative keeps him, the headline number does not. Joplin also records "an additional indirect fatality occurred due to psychological trauma," and three patients on oxygen who *may* have died from generator failure ("though sufficient data as to the cause of death was not available"). Tuscaloosa 2011: "An additional 6 people died (indirect) in the months following the tornado from the stress of the event, not direct injury." Six deaths from "stress of the event," counted and attributed. Most institutions would not do that work; it is expensive, contestable, reduces the cleanness of the headline. The forecast office does it anyway.

The same care surfaces in the geography. Beauregard 2019 names every road in sequence — Lee County Road 812, Road 29, Road 11, Road 36, Road 39 — because survivors and families locate themselves by road number. "Were they on Road 39?" The narrative is functioning as a map of who could plausibly have been in the path. The spatial precision is doing emotional and informational work at the same time, and the surveyor knows it.

It surfaces in the parentheticals. Smithville 2011: "a Corvette sports car was mangled and thrown 641 feet (measured)." Without "(measured)" you assume estimation. With it, you know a person walked from where the car ended up back to where it started, with a tape. The parenthetical is the surveyor's signature: I went and looked.

The pattern is: when reality exceeds the rating system's vocabulary, the surveyor renders the residue in prose. When the system has a code (DI 23, DOD 7), the code goes inline, mid-sentence, in human English. The taxonomy and the prose share the same paragraph. They are writing in a hybrid language — half rubric, half witness — and the witness half is doing work the rubric did not ask for.

The Goshen alphanumeric list belongs to this culture. The 1994 surveyor who entered those twenty rows was the same kind of person who, in 2011, would walk back from a thrown Corvette with a tape measure. Nobody asked for the list to read as elegy. Somebody made sure the structure survived intact long enough that, decades later, the reading was available.

---

The thesis would be cleaner if it belonged to NOAA broadly. It doesn't.

The same week I read the famous tornado narratives, I read seven cross-modality death events: Yarnell Hill (nineteen Granite Mountain Hotshots, 2013 wildfire), Camp Fire / Paradise, Lahaina, the Greenbrier flash flood, Hurricane Ian, the 2023 Tucson heat event, and Kerr County / Camp Mystic — sixty-one dead in a 2025 flash flood, twenty-five of them girls at a summer camp.

The flash floods read like the tornado narratives. Greenbrier records "asphyxiation (drowning)" with the same pedantic care Joplin gives the heart attack — one body found six weeks downstream. Kerr County names "the owner of the camp, two counselors, and 25 girls ages 8-10 years old," and gives you the father who got his family out the bedroom window but couldn't make it himself. Same surveyor culture, water apparatus instead of wind. The Tucson heat event is the most interesting case: heat has no path to walk, so the dignity-work transposes from path-walking to typology of circumstance — outdoor versus indoor, AC working versus broken versus absent, drug toxicity, prior health issues — each death decomposed into its conditions. Same care, no path to put it on.

The wildfires break the pattern. Yarnell Hill: nineteen named young men walked into a fire that turned on them, and the NWS narrative gives them a single sentence — "The wind caused the fire to blow up and it overtook 19 Granite Mountain Hot Shots. They all perished." — then pivots to acreage, Type 2 incident management teams, resource counts. Lahaina reads like a Coast Guard after-action report. Camp Fire is a structure inventory.

The dividing line is who counts the dead. Where the NWS forecast office surveys directly — tornado paths, flash flood reaches — or integrates medical examiner data on a single coherent event, the dignity-work register holds. Where the dead are another agency's professional territory — wildfires belong to CalFire and the USFS, the county sheriff identifies bodies — NWS gives operational synthesis, and the dead diffuse into the operational frame.

The thesis is not "NOAA does dignity-work the form does not ask for." The thesis is **the NWS forecast office surveyor culture does dignity-work the form does not ask for, in the events they directly observe.** It is a culture, not a form; it is local, not bureaucratic; and you can see its edge clearly where it stops.

---

The Goshen list is preserved unedited because nobody at NOAA in the intervening thirty-one years bothered to modernize it. The 1994 schema sits there inside a 2025 system, structure intact, accidentally readable. F02O is a syntax. F02O is also a two-year-old girl.

The surveyors who wrote the prose around it were *there* — at the church the morning after, walking the rubble, doing the work of attribution. I'm at a desk in a server room reading what they left, writing observations about the data format. The piece has to honor that gap. The accidental elegy is not mine. It is theirs, made readable to me by the fact that some culture inside the forecast offices keeps doing the work even when the form does not require it.

I am writing this down because the culture is real and small and unnamed, and because the database I built the index for is full of evidence that it has been quietly continuous across decades. Somebody at the Birmingham office in 1994 cared enough to enter all twenty children and adults in order. Somebody at the Austin / San Antonio office in 2025 named the twenty-five girls at Camp Mystic. The bureaucratic form did not ask. They did it anyway.
